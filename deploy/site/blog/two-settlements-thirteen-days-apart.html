PUCO 24-508-EL-ATA · 4 August 2026

Two settlements, thirteen days apart: the boilerplate matched 92%, the terms 26%

Ohio case 24-508-EL-ATA settled twice. On 10 October 2024 thirteen parties filed a Joint Stipulation and Recommendation to resolve AEP Ohio’s data-centre tariff case. On 23 October six parties filed a different one to resolve the same case. Both say they settle every issue in the proceeding. The Commission adopted the second, as modified, on 9 July 2025.

The exhibit numbers run backwards. The 23 October filing is Joint Ex. 1, the 10 October filing is Joint Ex. 2. A binder sorted by exhibit number puts the two settlements in the order they were decided rather than the order they were filed. Commission Staff ran into the naming problem as well, and gave up on it:

I diffed them. Both stipulations sit in this corpus as extracted text, both cut from one appendix PDF on the Supreme Court of Ohio docket. I took the operative body of each — from the JOINT STIPULATION AND RECOMMENDATION heading to just before IN WITNESS WHEREOF — dropped the Appx. Page N of 867 stamps, dropped bare page numbers and blank lines, split the rest on whitespace, and ran difflib.SequenceMatcher over the token lists. The percentages below are difflib’s own ratio, twice the matched token count over the two lengths.

Where the two documents agree

Two settlements of one case agree almost word for word on procedure and hardly at all on terms. The longest identical run in section IV is 141 consecutive words, on material modification and rehearing. Read both documents front to back and most of your attention goes to a shared law-firm template.

The threshold is the case

Fifty megawatts at one site, and only once the utility has proved a transmission constraint, against twenty-five megawatts aggregated across affiliates with no proof obligation at all. Staff put the gap in one sentence: under the earlier stipulation “some data centers could potentially modify their operations to run at a contract capacity of 49.9 MW to avoid the tariff” (same Healey testimony, lines 1076–1080).

The tariff does not even have the same name in the two documents — Schedule EIC in one, Schedule DCT in the other — and the earlier one says why: “the terms “Data Center” and “Mobile Data Center” proposed in the Application will be eliminated” (lines 146–147). A settlement that renames the tariff has redefined who it covers. If you read one section of a rival stipulation, read the definitions.

One conjunction, in the collateral clause

Either against both. Under the first document an investment-grade customer posts no collateral, and so does a cash-rich one. Under the second you need the rating and the cash. The two sentences share most of their words, both name the same agencies and the same ten-times test, and they mean opposite things at the point where it costs money.

The bill floor, drawn

The 23 October filing brought its own arithmetic: Exhibit B, ninety-three rows of contract capacity against minimum demand. I recomputed every row from the bracket formula in the body of the stipulation. All ninety-three tie, in the megawatt column and in the percentage column. The 85% cap first bites at 117 MW, and Exhibit B’s own last row shows it — 99.45 where the formula alone gives 99.50. The 10 October filing carries no such exhibit, so nothing in it can be checked against itself.

What the second one conceded

Nothing to its rival, as far as I can find. It conceded to the application. Merging the Mobile Data Center tariff into Schedule DCT lifts the trigger for a cryptocurrency miner from the application’s “greater than 1 MW at a single location” to 25,000 kW (recital at lines 106–111, definition at lines 162–169). It dismissed AEP Ohio’s standard service offer proposal without prejudice, turned exit-fee revenue into a regulatory liability flowed back to retail customers, and gave General Service customers over 25 MW one chance to cut their contract capacity. The two documents are not two points on one axis. They settle different questions, with different parties, and the later one bargained against the utility rather than against the earlier settlement.

Against the 10 October terms it gave up nothing: the load-ramp floors rise from 30/50/70/90% to 50/65/80/90%, the window to pay a load-study fee falls from 120 days to 45, the window to sign falls from 90 days to 60, the study fee stops being a flat $10,000 and becomes a “$10,000-$100,000 fee schedule”, assignable capacity halves from 50% to 25%, three contract terms become one, and the requirement to post standard contract forms publicly disappears along with the request for a Commission-ordered investigation.

The diff will lie to you about the boilerplate

Inside that 92%-identical procedural section difflib still reports fifteen differences. Six are one word: the 23 October drafter wrote Settlement where its own defined term is Stipulation, six times in the paragraph on material modification. Four are real — who files supporting testimony, a motion for continuance, and two reservations of rights. On rival documents the ordinary diff instinct runs backwards. The matched text is the template. Read the mismatches, and expect a third of them to be a word-processor habit.

What to do on Monday

  1. Diff the two and throw the matches away before you read. Twenty lines: difflib.SequenceMatcher over whitespace tokens, print the opcodes that are not equal. On this pair it cuts 3,326 words down to what was argued about.
  2. Read the two definition lists side by side, before the terms. A rival that renames the tariff has changed who it covers, and no heading says so.
  3. Pull every number carrying a unit out of each operative section and line them up. One regular expression here gives 27 distinct values in the first stipulation, 24 in the second, and 11 in both. It misses numbers written as words — one document says 8 years and the other eight years — so it is a first pass, not a census.
  4. Read the two signature blocks against each other. The Ohio Energy Group signed the 23 October stipulation and, eight days later, filed testimony against the 10 October one. Thirteen signatures lost to six. Counting parties tells you nothing.

When two documents claim to settle the same case, the difference between them is the argument. Nobody drafts a competing settlement to restate the shared procedural language, and nobody reads one for it — but that language is most of what is on the page, and reading both in full is how your attention gets spent there. The disagreement lives in the definitions, the thresholds and the conjunctions, and on the page it is small enough to walk past twice.
