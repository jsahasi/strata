The five, in full

If you rehearse nothing else, rehearse these. Between them they answer all four evaluation dimensions.

WarmupYour citation verification runs when a page is read, not when the claim is written. Why that way round, and what does it cost you?

Because a write-time verdict is a promise about bytes that can move afterwards. verified_claims re-reads the stored source at the cited offsets every time a claim renders. Edit the source and the claim flips to withheld on the next page load. No job runs, nothing rebuilds, the page just refuses. What it costs I measured this morning rather than guessed. The cost is linear in the document, not in the quote: about nine milliseconds on a fifty-three kilobyte filing, a hundred and seventy on the million-character Kentucky testimony. Nearly all of it is the ambiguity check, which projects the whole document. The string comparison itself is forty microseconds.

Evidence. app/state/claims.py:502 (verified_claims); app/verification/verifier.py:151-201. Measured on this machine: occurrence_count 8.9 ms on va-scc-PUR-2026-00056-odec-dembek-corrected-testimony-clean-redline.txt (53,132 chars); verify_citation 168.7 ms on ky-2025-00113-kollen-direct-testimony-corrected-clean.txt (1,024,536 chars), of which occurrence_count alone is 169.5 ms.

CoreWhere does modern AI fail, and how did you design around it?

It fails by being fluently, plausibly, subtly wrong in a way nothing downstream can detect. A model reads a paragraph well and remembers where it read it badly. It hands you a quotation that reads exactly like a commission order and is not in the order, and nothing in the shape of the answer says so. No confidence dip, no hedge. So the design assumes misquotation and makes it survivable rather than trying to prompt it away. Every claim carries version, offsets and quoted text, and the source is re-read before anything is shown. A claim that fails is withheld, not greyed out. And nothing is repaired. Snap an offset to the nearest match and the gate is checking the repair.

Evidence. ADR-03 at docs/.ai/decisions.html:54-59; app/state/claims.py:302-322 — WithheldClaim is frozen with slots=True and has no statement field, so the template cannot render one by accident; app/web/templates/change.html:4-9; app/interpretation/propose.py:67-71 ("NOTHING IS REPAIRED").

WarmupWho is this for? One person.

The regulatory affairs analyst at a multi-state investor-owned utility. The person who opens the filing, works out what changed, and tells the business what it now has to do. Not counsel, who bills by the hour for exactly that reading and has the opposite incentive. Not the compliance officer, who owns the obligation register but consumes the analysis rather than producing it. Not the executive. And not regulated enterprises, which is an industry, not a person. That is ADR-01, written on day one with the alternatives beside it. It is a hypothesis rather than a finding, because nobody has confirmed it. If two interviews say counsel does the real reading, the ADR gets rewritten and the PRD with it. It says that in the ADR.

1. The gate — citation verification

The read-path question and its cost live in the five. Start there, then these two.

CoreThere is no stored "verified" column. But a hash of the source would tell you the document has not changed. Why would that not do instead?

Because a hash answers a different question. A matching hash says the whole document is unchanged. It does not say the quote sits at those offsets, so you still have to read them. And a mismatch is too blunt the other way: one unrelated typo would withhold every claim in that filing. So the hash is an addition, not a replacement. I do hash, in verify_citation_for_version, and it costs well under a millisecond on a megabyte. But nothing under app calls that function. Only tests do, and I checked with grep before saying so. Drift is caught on cited spans and missed everywhere else in the same filing. That is a real hole and the docstring says it rather than hiding it.

Evidence. app/state/claims.py:469-499 (the docstring names the gap); app/verification/verifier.py:204-262, hash at :255-260. Measured: sha256 of the 1,024,536-char file, 0.57 ms.

KillerThe quote a claim cites appears twice in the filing. What happens?

The claim is refused. The verifier counts occurrences, and if there is more than one, the citation has to state which one it rests on or nothing is asserted. The same sentence in two sections is two different obligations. This is not a synthetic problem. In the Kentucky testimony we ingested, the phrase Cane Run BESS appears four times. I ran it just now: cited with no occurrence it refuses, cited as occurrence zero it verifies, cited as occurrence one it refuses. The synthetic corpus plants the same trap on purpose. And the tempting fix is the one we refuse. We could derive the occurrence from the offsets the model gave us. That check would agree with itself every time.

Evidence. app/verification/verifier.py:137-148, 196-199; tests/test_occurrence.py; app/interpretation/propose.py:72-80. Measured live: occurrence_count("Cane Run BESS") is 4; occurrence 0 verifies; no occurrence and occurrence 1 both refuse.

2. The model — what it may do, and where it has run

"Where does modern AI fail" is in the five.

HardWhat is the model actually allowed to do in this product?

One thing. It is shown a single change the deterministic diff already found — both sides, the section, the exact offsets — and asked whether it matters to the utility, in one sentence, with a citation. It is never asked what changed. The diff knows that exactly and can be tested against known answers. It is never asked whether a version is draft or final; that is read from a column and stated to it as fact. Its answer then passes five structural drops before the verifier is reached: malformed, a version this change does not span, an empty quote, offsets out of range, and a span outside the change it was shown. That last one matters most. A citation can verify perfectly and still be about text the model was never handed.

Evidence. app/interpretation/propose.py:82-88 (draft or final read from the field, ADR-05), :194-197 (the named fallbacks), :214-231 (the DROP_ codes including DROP_OUTSIDE_THE_CHANGE); ADR-04 and ADR-05 in decisions.html.

KillerYou keep saying the live model path ran once. Is that true?

No, and the page says so before you do. A second request almost certainly went out on the same day. An agent ran the probe script with send, believing the key was absent because it had cleared the environment variable — but load_env puts any name from the dot-env file back that is not already set, which is exactly what clearing it creates. The output was piped to head and lost. So I cannot tell you the response, the token counts, or whether it succeeded. What I can defend is the one deliberate call: request bytes, response fields, stop reason, usage, cost, all written down, and a test that holds the request block to the exact bytes so it cannot be quietly edited.

Evidence. docs/.ai/live-transport-probe.html:34 — the correction note sits directly under the headline sentence and names the second call. tests/test_live_transport_probe.py:288 rebuilds the request through AnthropicTransport and asserts it byte for byte.

3. The deterministic half — diff, mapping, retrieval

HardYour diff is difflib. Where does it break?

On wholesale restructuring. Sequence alignment sees a section renumbered and relocated as a deletion plus an addition, and the module says so rather than pretending. The dangerous part is subtler: text similarity runs high exactly when structural identity has changed. Our corpus carries the case on purpose. Section 6 becomes subsection 5.4 and the words barely move. I measured it a few minutes ago: raw similarity is 0.944. Report that as a confident match and a restructure looks like a small edit, which is how a moved obligation gets missed. So a disagreement in the leading section number caps the score at 0.5, below any plausible escalation threshold. It is a ceiling, not a judgement. The alignment became an inference, and an inference escalates.

Evidence. app/diff/engine.py:1-11 (the weakness, disclosed), :22 (RESTRUCTURE_CONFIDENCE_CEILING = 0.5), :53-75 (_alignment_confidence); data/manifest.json CHG-5. Measured live: _similarity 0.9443, _alignment_confidence 0.5000, labels "6" and "5.4".

HardRetrieval. Why FTS5, and what happens when the index is wrong?

FTS5 is compiled into the SQLite the app already opens, so there is no service, no process, nothing for a reviewer to install before the run command works. More important, the index points at passage rows that already carry version, ordinal and character offsets, so a hit is already in citation shape and composes straight into the verifier. Most retrieval chunks text and then cannot cite what it retrieved. When the index is missing, stale, or unavailable, the read does not return fewer results. It falls back to a full scan of every passage in scope and says which path answered and why, because a short list with no announcement is the worst outcome available. And every hit is checked against its fingerprint.

Evidence. app/state/search.py:27-40 (why FTS5, why Passage), :62-88 (the three named degradations and what the staleness check misses); app/state/queries.py:258-277 (the whole-corpus fallback). ADR-71 at decisions.html:689-696 records 366 ms scanning against 1.3 ms indexed on 8,707 passages.

HardThe brief asks you to map a change to the documents it affects. Where is that?

It does not exist, and the schema says so out loud. An obligation carries a source document reference — the string DOC-2 — and the comment beside it says: not a foreign key, those documents have no table, they are entries in a JSON file, and naming the column with an id suffix would promise a join nothing can satisfy. The four company documents are seeded as Source rows with a locator into that JSON and no body text at all. So there is no document to re-read, no offsets, no verification. The chain I actually have is change to obligation to a document label. Calling that document mapping would be exactly the fluent unverified assertion this product exists to refuse.

Evidence. app/state/models.py:1513-1518 (the comment and the column); app/seed.py seeds the four documents as Source rows with locator data/company_context.json#DOC-n. data/company_context.json holds 4 documents and 8 obligations.

KillerYour change-to-obligation mapping is a word overlap. That is barely AI. Why is it in the product at all?

Because the alternative was worse. Nothing wrote that table, so every change in the product refused with no obligation mapped. And it is deliberately not the semantic join. ADR-08 reserves that for embeddings I did not build, and the company context is arranged to defeat anything less: one duty says post security where the docket says post collateral. Four rules, each measured against the corpus. Four characters or more. Prefix match, not substring, because work sits inside network and every network upgrade was proposing the security duty. A word more than half the company's duties use is not evidence. And two words, not one. The part that matters is downstream: a mapping the pipeline proposed cannot put work on a person's desk.

Evidence. app/state/mapping.py:1-10 (the hole it fills), :12-27 (ADR-08 and note_for_semantic_join), :55-88 (the four rules, with "work" inside "network" and "large" in six of eight); app/state/search.py:259 (PREFIX_MIN = 4); app/state/routing.py:184 and :940-975 (ROUTE_MAPPING_UNCONFIRMED; the candidate goes in candidate_user_ids and never user_id).

4. The record — audit chain and tenancy

CoreTenant isolation. Why a chokepoint rather than a code review rule?

Because a rule three people have to remember fails on the fourth call site, and it already did here. There are two guards and they answer different questions. The first refuses a company id that could match more than the caller meant: blank, whitespace, padded, or carrying a wildcard. That is the way in. The second is the way out, and it exists because the first got mistaken for it. One function called the scope guard, passed because the string was well formed, then fetched by primary key and handed back whatever row carried that id — another tenant's included. Ids are unique across tenants and printed on the front of filings. Now the fetch and the check are one call. And another tenant's row raises rather than answering None, because None collapses nobody holds this into somebody else does.

Evidence. app/state/queries.py:1-23 (the two-guards docstring, naming routing.py::ensure_obligation as the failing site), :57 (SCOPE_WILDCARDS), :60-107 (_require_scope), :113-172 (row_for_company, CrossTenantRow at :168).

CoreYour audit log carries three digest schemes. Why not migrate them to one?

Because migrating them would destroy the thing they exist to prove. Scheme one hashes the ten original fields. Scheme two adds who acted, from where, under which session. Scheme three adds the reversal pointer. Rows written before attribution existed still verify under scheme one, forever, and nothing rewrites them. Re-hash them under the newest scheme and you get a chain that verifies and proves nothing, because the process that verified it is the process that rewrote it. So each row states its own scheme in a column and verification dispatches per row. A chain mixing all three verifies end to end, because the previous hash is a value copied forward and does not know which function made it. The migration adds columns and backfills the version number. It never touches a hashed field.

Evidence. app/state/audit.py:13-24 (module docstring), :95-126 (the scheme comment, CURRENT_DIGEST_VERSION = DIGEST_V2 at :126), :262 / :309 / :369 (the three digest functions), :430-506 (_digest_for_row), :691 (verify_chain), :799-820 (migrate_audit_schema: "no rehash step and there must never be one", :804).

KillerWhat is a reversal pointer, and what stops somebody writing one onto an old row?

Undo is a row, not an edit. Taking back a decision appends an event naming the one it reverses. The reverted row is not edited, not deleted, not flagged, because the record of a correction is exactly what an auditor comes for. The pointer sits inside the hash, in scheme three, for the same reason attribution does: a column beside the hash can be re-pointed while the chain still verifies, and the log would then assert with a valid hash that a decision nobody undid had been undone. Now your question. Schemes one and two do not hash that column. So a pointer on such a row is an assertion nothing defends, and the verifier refuses that row outright. Application code cannot make one. A row in that state arrived out of band.

Evidence. app/state/audit.py:26-32 (rollback is a row), :109-116 (the hole and how it is closed), :126 (CURRENT_DIGEST_VERSION stays at 2), :369-427 (_digest_v3), :430-453 (_digest_for_row raises AuditTamperError on a v1 or v2 row carrying a pointer), :216-217 (the two undo action codes).

KillerYou have two thousand tests. How do you know the tenant filters are actually there, rather than the tests passing for other reasons?

I checked, and the honest answer is that they were mostly not proving it. I deleted eighteen tenant filters one at a time and ran the suite after each. The two hand-written isolation files caught four — the four functions they name. One deletion turned nothing red at all. That is two thousand tests answering "is the guard there" with a shrug. So the guard is derived from the code now. Three sweeps: every scoped function called with a foreign scope; a syntax-tree pass requiring every query under app to name the company; and every mounted route driven signed in as one company. Asking per query rather than per function took it from catching seventy-eight of a hundred and seventy-six deletions to a hundred and one. Not all of them.

Evidence. tests/test_tenancy_derived.py:1-42 (the eighteen-deletion audit and the function nothing caught, queries.py::passage_counts_by_version), :795-801 (78 of 176 to 101), :822 (AST_EXEMPT is empty), :840 (AST_QUERY_EXEMPT: 8 entries, 11 allowed queries, each with its reason).

5. Scale — what gives first

"What is the one thing you would rewrite" is in the five, and it is the same defect seen from the other end.

KillerTake the corpus to a hundred times its size. What gives first, and where exactly?

The claims read, and I can point at the line. The function that builds the source map loads every version the company owns, whole source text, on every change render. Today that corpus is a hundred and two filings, fifteen megabytes. At a hundred times it is about one and a half gigabytes pulled into memory to draw one page. That goes before anything else does. Second is verification cost per citation, which is linear in the document rather than the corpus: nine milliseconds on fifty-three kilobytes, a hundred and seventy on a megabyte. More filings do not make that worse; bigger ones do. Third is the retrieval fallback, which is a whole-corpus scan by design. The first two share one fix.

Evidence. app/state/claims.py:469-499 (_sources_by_version reads versions_for_company entire); app/verification/verifier.py:151-201; app/state/queries.py:258-277. Measured: data/real is 102 files, 15 MB; 8.9 ms on 53,132 chars, 168.7 ms on 1,024,536 chars.

6. The user

"Who is this for" and "did you talk to real users" are both in the five. These four come after them.

CoreThe compliance officer owns the obligation register. Isn't she the buyer and the user?

She may well be the buyer. She is not the user. The register is a list of duties already agreed; the expensive work is turning a new version of a proceeding into a judgement about whether one of those duties just moved. That is production, not consumption, and the analyst does it. I keep exactly one other role in the model, and it is not a second user — the obligation owner, who approves the action the analyst recommends. That separation is a security control, because the person who interprets a change should not be the person who signs off acting on it. Where the demo lets you self-approve, I call that a disclosed downgrade rather than the design.

Evidence. docs/.ai/decisions.html:42 (ADR-01's second role, named as load-bearing and not a second user); app/state/routing.py:203. Role permissions read live from strata.db: analyst holds action.propose; obligation_owner holds action.approve and action.reject; admin holds neither.

CoreWalk me through their day. Where exactly does this help?

Several open proceedings at once, in several states, because the utility operates in several. Months of quiet, then a draft order posts, then a short comment window, then a final order with a deadline attached. Filings arrive as PDFs by email against a service list, often scanned, two copies of everything — one public, one confidential. The moment I built for is narrow. A second version of something she has already read lands. She does not want a summary; she knows the old one. She wants the delta in the source's own words, whether it binds yet, which of her obligations it touches, and who needs to see it this week. Today that is two documents side by side and a redline built by eye. This is a model, not an observation. Nobody has walked me through last Tuesday.

Evidence. docs/prd.html:60-63, and the section is headed "modeled, not yet observed" with its own honesty check at :61; docs/synthetic-user.html:61-62.

KillerYou wrote the persona, from filings you picked, for a product you built. Why is that worth anything?

Its agreement is worth nothing and I say so on the page. It was assembled by the same mind, from a corpus that same mind chose, so it produces the pains the product already claims to solve. Two things make it non-zero. Every trait carries a file and a line in a real filing, so a reader can check the trait rather than trust me, and the traits I could not source are tagged as guesses with what would kill them. And it earned its place by failing. Asked to walk through last Tuesday it had nothing, so that question survived onto the real script. A question a synthetic cannot answer is exactly the one worth a stranger's ten minutes. The corpus is also biased on purpose. I selected for errata and version pairs.

Evidence. docs/synthetic-user.html:53 (bias on purpose), :66 ("A trait with no source does not appear"), :252-253 ("It will agree with the product too readily"); docs/prd.html:88-89; docs/user-research.html (the rehearsal cut four questions and produced the two marked "can hurt").

KillerOf the last ten things that landed on her desk, how many had an earlier version she had already read?

I don't know, and if the answer is two then my unit of work is aimed at the wrong moment. That question came out of rehearsing against the synthetic persona and it is the sharpest thing the rehearsal produced, because the product only makes a change when it has two versions to compare. The first version of any proceeding has nothing to diff against, and nothing in this build fills that gap — no branch for it in the design, the data model or the board. If most arrivals are cold, then cold start is not a gap in the product, it is the product, and the whole change-centric decision gets rewritten rather than patched. It is question two on my call script, marked as one of the two that can hurt.

Evidence. docs/prd.html:87-89 (cold start, and "It may be worse than a weakness"); docs/user-research.html:239-240 (question 2, tagged as able to hurt ADR-002).

HardWhat did reading 102 filings teach you that you did not expect?

Three things, and the first two changed the design. The redline lies. A Utah errata redline shows a page reference of eighty-six where both the original and the clean copy read eight — a change that never happened — and it silently passed over one that did. So the wedge is not producing a redline, it is ranking one. Second, a diff cannot see what is not there. A Missouri corrected stipulation is fifteen lines longer than the original, the prose identical, and the added lines are the rate table. The document that sets the prices had been filed without the prices. 9. Security, and the code nothing calls

CoreYou have no CSRF token anywhere in this application. Every admin form is forgeable.

There is no token, correct. SameSite=Lax on the session cookie is the whole defence, and I wrote that in the modules that own forms rather than leaving it to be discovered. What that buys and what it does not: Lax stops a cross-site POST from carrying the cookie, which covers the ordinary form-forgery shape, and it does nothing against an attacker who can get script onto my own origin. The login form has no token either, so somebody can make a victim's browser sign in as the attacker. There is a per-account lockout — five wrong passwords, fifteen minutes — but nothing throttles an attacker spreading guesses across many addresses. That belongs at the edge and this build has no edge.

Evidence. app/web/deps.py:78-79 — the comment says Lax "is the whole of the CSRF defence on this build — there is no token"; app/auth/sessions.py:45; app/auth/sessions.py:101-102 (MAX_FAILED_ATTEMPTS = 5, LOCKOUT 15 minutes). CoreYour demo signs anybody in as an administrator with one click and no password. Walk me through what that account can do.

Yes, and it is my call — ADR-84. One unauthenticated POST to the demo login returns a session. Known limits

Every limit below is a fact about the system, stated here rather than left to be found. Each one is checkable in the tree.

Nobody has been interviewed. Zero. Forty-five messages, four bounces, no replies. User empathy is the weak dimension in this submission and no amount of further engineering moves it.

The persona is invented and labelled so. Its agreement is worth nothing. Its traceability is worth something. Never offer it as a substitute for a conversation.

The gate checks the words, not the reading. Verified means the words exist where the claim says they do. A fluent, correctly-cited, wrong reading passes.

Two controls are built, tested, and not wired. can_approve refuses self-approval and no view calls it. start_run begins an approval run and nothing in app/ calls it.

The approval engine rests on the weakest row in the persona. Largest body of code, tagged ASSUMED, and its clock is off by default.
