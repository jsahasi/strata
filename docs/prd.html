Strata — Product Requirements

A citation-grade regulatory change-intelligence workspace, built for one user. Living document — updated as the build and the user interviews change what we know, not rewritten at the end.

Where this document describes a step as designed rather than running, it says so in the step.

90-second pitch

Strata is regulatory change intelligence for one person: the regulatory affairs analyst at a multi-state investor-owned utility, the one who reads a new docket filing and has to work out, fast, what changed, whether it is binding yet, and what the business must now do.

Today that work is manual. A new draft or order lands; the analyst opens it beside the last version, finds the delta by eye, judges materiality against memory of the obligations register, and routes it by hallway conversation or email. An alert only tells them something moved. Search only helps if they already know what to look for. Neither one does the interpretation, and the interpretation is the expensive part.

Strata takes two versions of a proceeding and diffs them deterministically. A model is never asked to find the change; it is only ever asked to say something about a change it has been shown. Every claim carries a citation to an exact source passage, and that citation is verified against the stored text before the claim is ever shown as fact. An unverified claim does not assert itself; it goes to review, labelled as unverified, not hidden.

The hard part is exactly that: language models misquote fluently and with confidence, and in this domain a wrong citation is worse than no answer. Strata assumes the model will get it wrong sometimes and is built so that failure is visible and survivable, not invisible.

The demonstration runs on a real filing, once scripts/ingest_real.py is run against it — nothing invokes that script automatically, so a fresh database holds the synthetic corpus only. Kentucky PSC docket 2025-00113, a witness's direct testimony filed and then corrected by the filer: 1,024,409 characters against 1,024,536, a 127-character difference across a million. The pipeline — segment, diff and persist — finds 144 changes in 0.78 seconds. That is the problem stated as arithmetic rather than as a claim, and because the filer also published their own marked-up copy, there is an answer key nobody on this side wrote.

The user

One person, per ADR-001: the regulatory affairs analyst at a multi-state investor-owned utility. Not the compliance officer, who owns the obligation register but consumes analysis rather than producing it. Not outside counsel, who bills by the hour for exactly this work and has no incentive to make it faster. Not "regulated enterprises" in general, which describes an industry, not a person.

The analyst is the one who reads the docket. Their job is to turn a filing into a judgement — what changed, whether it matters, whether it is final — and to hand that judgement to the people who act on it. That is the job Strata is built to make faster and more defensible, not the job of filing comments or writing legal opinions.

A day in the analyst's week — modeled, not yet observed

Honesty check. The workflow below is the model Strata is designed against, not an observation. No interview has been completed as of this writing — that has not changed, and no amount of further engineering changes it. What has changed is the evidence underneath the model: it used to rest on how public-utility-commission dockets work as a matter of public record plus reasoning about who touches each step. It now also rests on data/real/ — 102 filings from eight commissions, filed into live proceedings by utilities, intervenors and commission staff, each record naming its filer, docket and date. Be careful what that buys: the filer is an organisation on every record and only 19 name a witness, so the corpus shows the work of this job without often naming the person who did it. That is better than reasoning from first principles and it is still not a conversation. A filing shows what someone produced; it cannot show what it cost them, how often, or what they nearly missed. H1 and H2 below (see "User feedback") test the two load-bearing claims here directly, and this section gets rewritten the moment a real conversation contradicts it.

The analyst tracks several open proceedings at once, across several state commissions, because the utility operates in more than one jurisdiction. Proceedings move slowly and then suddenly — months of quiet, then a draft order posts, then a short comment window, then a final order with a compliance deadline attached. The analyst's job inside that rhythm is to notice the version that matters, work out what it changed from the last one, and decide, this week, whether the business has to do something and by when.

The moment Strata is built for: a new version of a proceeding the utility is a party to appears. The analyst does not want a summary of the new document — they have read the old one and know it well. They want the delta: what changed, in the source's own words, whether it is a draft they should watch or a final order they must comply with, which of the utility's existing obligations or projects it touches, and who on their team needs to see it this week. Today that delta is assembled by hand: two documents open side by side, a redline built by eye, a judgement of materiality made from memory, and a routing decision made by knowing who owns what. Strata's job is to produce that same delta as a verified, citable artefact instead of a manual reconstruction.

Why alerts and search are not enough

Both exist today and neither does the analyst's actual work.

An alert says something moved. A docket-tracking subscription or a law firm client memo tells the analyst a new filing posted. It does not tell them what changed inside it, whether the change is material, whether it is still a draft that could be withdrawn on rehearing, or which of the utility's obligations it touches. The analyst still has to open both versions and do the entire interpretive job the alert implied was done. An alert converts silence into a to-do item; it does not shrink the to-do item.

Search is worse, because it assumes the question. Search answers "where does this proceeding mention interconnection timelines" — a query the analyst can only write if they already know a change touched interconnection timelines. The analyst's actual starting point is the opposite: they do not know what changed yet, that is the thing they are trying to find out. A tool that requires the query to already contain the answer is not built for the moment it claims to serve.

What both tools skip is the same step: turning a new version into a judged, cited, routed piece of work. That step is the product. It is why the unit of work in Strata (ADR-002) is a change, carried through to a recommended action with a named reviewer — not a document, not a query result, not a notification.

An assistant did ship, so square that with the paragraph above. Clarke reads the workspace record and only the record. ADR-050 makes it a commission's records clerk rather than a guide: ask whether a change is material and it says that is the analyst's call, then offers what it holds — the versions, the diff, the obligation, the owner. Asked for something not on the record, it says so without embarrassment, which is ADR-003 in human form. A tool that can withhold has to return its withheld count, and a missing count stops the turn rather than letting it report zero (ADR-052). None of that makes search the wedge; it makes the record reachable by a person who would rather type a sentence than learn a screen.

The wedge, end to end

One workflow, deliberately narrow, run against two corpora that do different jobs. The synthetic proceeding — three versions, known material and immaterial edits, plus a synthetic company context of obligations, projects and documents (docs/.ai/tasks.html, T3) — carries traps built on purpose and is what the evals measure against, because a real filing will not reproduce a trap on demand. The real corpus in data/real/ is the demonstration: a filing somebody actually made, with the commission's own URL beside it. ADR-040 decided that split and refused to let either corpus stand in for the other.

Each step below says whether it runs. runs the code does this today. partly some of it runs and the gap is named. designed, not built the decision is recorded and no code does it. This follows the rule the diagrams already obey (ADR-041): work that is designed and not built must never be drawn like work that ships. A wedge diagram in which every box looks alike is the drawing equivalent of a claim asserted on a citation nobody checked.
  1. runsIngest two versions of a proceeding. Each is segmented into passages with stable character offsets and a hash of the source text — the foundation everything downstream cites back to. app/ingestion/ingest.py never rewrites the text it stores, and app/pipeline.py refuses to re-ingest a version id whose stored bytes have moved. Note the limit of that: the hash is checked when bytes are offered, not on the read path. The one read-side function that re-hashes, verify_citation_for_version, says in its own docstring that nothing calls it.
  2. runsDiff, deterministically. Segments are aligned and compared with an exact, testable algorithm — difflib.SequenceMatcher over normalised passages in app/diff/engine.py, no model, no clock, no randomness. The model is not asked to find changes (ADR-004): diffing has a known right answer per test fixture, and asking a model to do it too would make the step untestable. Alignment confidence is capped when the section label disagrees, so a renumbering escalates instead of scoring 0.944 and presenting itself as a confident match.
  3. runsInterpret materiality. For each typed change the model is shown that change — never either version's full text, which is the deterministic diff's job under ADR-004 — and asked only whether it is material and why, citing exact offsets. app/state/models.py keeps the verdict in two homes: materiality_for_company() earns one, shown_materiality_for_company() shows one. The verdict is stored and audited; the citation behind it is NOT stored and is re-read on every render, so a source edited afterwards withholds the verdict rather than repeating it (ADR-86).
  4. runsVerify the citation. Before any claim is shown as established, the verifier re-reads the stored source at the cited offsets and confirms the quote matches after normalisation — equality, not similarity (ADR-003). If it does not match, the claim is never displayed as fact: it goes to review, labelled unverified, with the reason shown. Two properties are worth more than the mechanism. The verdict is computed at read time and never stored, so a claim cannot inherit a verification it earned yesterday. And a repeated quote must name which occurrence it relies on or it does not verify — the synthetic corpus repeats one sentence three times per version precisely so that hole is testable rather than theoretical. A reviewer may dispute a refusal; nobody can override it, and there is no function in app/ that would let them (ADR-024).
  5. runsClassify draft versus final. This is a field on the version, never inferred at read time (ADR-005). Ingestion refuses a version that is neither. The status is copied onto the change when it is written and dispatched on in app/interpretation/action.py, so the two never share a code path — conflating them is the costliest error in the domain in both directions: acting on a draft that never survives comment, or missing a binding deadline because a final order was treated as provisional.
  6. built, and the join is lexicalMap impact. The change is matched against the company's obligations by the words they share, and a person confirms. app/state/mapping.py proposes; map_change_to_obligation writes; ChangeObligation.mapped_by_kind records whether the pipeline or a named analyst decided. After make seed: 26 mappings over 171 changes, 24 proposed by the pipeline and 2 confirmed by a person. Only the 2 confirmed ones route to a named owner. What is still deferred is the semantic half — OBL-001 says "post security" where the docket says "post collateral", and no lexical rule reaches that. ADR-008 reserves it for embeddings; ADR-85 records what shipped instead and why the shape (propose, show the words, let a person confirm) survives the day embeddings land. This entry read "designed, not built" until 2026-08-05, which was true and is the reason the step is now the one with the most evidence behind it.
  7. partlyRecommend an action and a reviewer. The reviewer half is real and careful. Routing walks change → obligation → owner and returns a named refusal rather than a guess when it cannot: no obligation, obligation unowned, owners disagree, owner unknown, owner inactive. There is also one outcome that is not a refusal — the owner is named but has not accepted their invitation, so the item is on a desk nobody can act from yet. An escalation that cannot be routed stays visibly unrouted in the shared queue instead of vanishing (ADR-036, ADR-056). A mapping nobody confirmed cannot put a person's name on work. Where the pipeline proposed the change-to-obligation link from a word overlap, routing answers ROUTE_MAPPING_UNCONFIRMED and returns the person it would have named as a candidate rather than as the owner (ADR-087). On the seeded corpus the product therefore declines to route 24 of the 26 mapped changes, and every unmapped change besides. That is the correct answer and it is a quieter demonstration: the reviewer sees a product refusing to assign rather than a product assigning. The refusal has a cure on the same screen — an analyst confirms or rejects a proposed mapping in one click, and the audit row records which of the two decided. The action half is not surfaced: the monitor, comment and comply vocabulary exists in code and no screen renders it, and no effective date is stored on anything — the phrase "with the effective date attached" was a design intention this document stated as fact, and it was wrong.
  8. partlyUpdate project state, with an audit trail. The audit trail is the strong part: append-only, hash-linked, carrying who acted and from which session, defended by a session guard against application writes and by the chain against out-of-band rewrites. Knowledge is superseded and never edited, so "what did we believe in March" stays answerable (ADR-031). What does not yet run is the confidence gate this step describes. The threshold ships at zero and there is no screen that sets it, so nothing is withheld for low confidence alone; what withholds today is the citation verifier, which is a stricter test but a narrower one. Absence is denial holds where it is implemented — a failure to verify degrades to a review item, never to a confident guess — and the confidence half of the rule (ADR-006) is configuration waiting for evidence, not a control that has fired.
  9. the reader runs, nothing mintsHand one claim to somebody with no account. The person who can approve acting on a change sits in Legal or in Rates, holds no login here, and stands at the end of every run of this workflow. What they get today is a quote pasted into an email, which they take on faith — the exact thing this product exists to replace, happening in the last step of the work, every time. A share link (ADR-72) carries one claim or one change to /s/<token>, readable with no account at all. Five controls stand where the session guard stood, each one a column rather than a convention: a token of 32 random bytes stored only as a SHA-256 digest, so a read of share_links hands nobody a working link; one artifact, never a project, never a proceeding, never a list; an expiry, seven days by default and thirty at most, never absent; revocation at any time; and an audit row for every mint, every open with its address and verdict, and every withdrawal, in the same hash-chained log as everything else. What makes this a product argument and not a convenience: open_share() calls verified_claims(), so the citation is re-read against the stored source during that request. Nothing rendered is stored and served back and there is no cached verdict to go stale, so a source edited after the link went out takes the statement off the page on the very next open — and the person watching a claim withdraw itself is outside the company. The gap is the mint. Nothing in app/ calls create_share_link(). The reader at /s/<token> is wired and public, the register at /admin/shares lists every link with its opens and stops any of them in one click, and no screen creates one — so a fresh database holds no link to open. Revocation has the same shape: the module lets the sharer withdraw their own link, and the only screen that offers the act belongs to an administrator.

The end state the analyst sees today: a list of changes, each with its citation and its draft-or-final status, a named reason wherever routing declines to name a reviewer — which, on the seeded corpus, is every time, because nothing writes the change-to-obligation mapping the walk starts from — and, for any claim whose quote does not match its source, a review item instead of a silent guess. What that list does not yet carry is a materiality judgement, a recommended action, or an effective date. Steps 3, 6 and 9 are the difference between the product described here and the product that runs, and naming which they are is more useful than a paragraph claiming all nine.

After the handoff: the approval route

The complaint about the step after this one is that an item disappears. The analyst hands it off and cannot say who has it, how long that person has had it, what happens if nobody acts, or what comes next — so they chase it by email, which is the workflow this product claims to replace. ADR-48 answers with two screens and two routers, deliberately not one screen with its buttons greyed out. The two questions have different shapes, and greying out the editor would hand the larger audience the shape built for the smaller one and call it access.

GET /workflow is the read-only half, open to anyone signed in, because the people who do not draw the route are the ones who have to be able to read it. It answers "where is my item and what happens next" as a numbered list of steps in the order an item meets them, each written as a sentence: on_timeout: escalate becomes "after 24 hours with no answer, this goes to anyone holding the Regulatory Affairs role". Every one of those translations lives in app/web/views/workflow.py and none of it in the template, so there is one place to check that no column value leaks into prose. The page carries no JavaScript at all — the one screen every signed-in person depends on must not be breakable by a script failing to load — and it does not soften a bypass: "after 48 hours this step is skipped and the item moves on without approval", with a step already skipped on a live item drawn at the same weight as an approved one and said to have been skipped rather than signed. That is the citation rule moved onto routing. A route that draws a bypassed step to look like an approved one is the same defect as a claim asserted on a citation that did not verify.

The /admin/workflows paths in app/web/views/admin.py are the other half, behind the workflow.manage permission, and they are a canvas of nodes and arrows because that is how you draw a graph. A save validates types and vocabulary and refuses a word the product does not have; activation validates completeness, and resolves every assignee rule and every escalation target against the company's roles and users, refusing the ones that land nowhere. role:approvers is well formed and, in a company with no such role, routes into silence — so absence is denial applies to routing exactly as it applies to citations. What neither screen can hide: nothing in this build creates a WorkflowRun. The tables exist, both screens read them, and no engine walks a route, so the "where has my item reached" half says no run has been started rather than rendering an empty list that reads as "nothing has happened".

Cold start

This wedge needs two versions of a proceeding before it produces a change. The first version of any proceeding Strata sees has nothing to diff against, and today nothing fills that gap. Running extraction and citation verification on a single version standalone — so the analyst gets a verified reading of the document before a second version exists to diff against — is the plan, not the build: no branch for it exists in the TDD, the architecture, the data model, or the task board. Cold start is a real weakness of a change-centric design, and the honest state of it is that it is planned, not yet designed. Naming it here, rather than claiming a fix the code does not back, is the stronger move.

It may be worse than a weakness. Rehearsing the interview against a synthetic persona produced one question nobody had thought to ask: of the last ten things that landed on you, how many had an earlier version you had already read? If the answer is two, the change-diff wedge is a minority case and cold start is not a gap in the product — it is the product, and ADR-002 is aimed at the wrong moment. It is now the fourth of the eight questions the rehearsal left standing. Note what it is and is not: a question worth asking, generated by a model from public filings, recorded in docs/synthetic-interview.html. It is not a finding, nobody said it, and it will not be treated as evidence for or against anything until a real analyst answers it.

What Strata deliberately does not do

  • Does not file anything. It recommends an action and a reviewer; a human files the comment, signs the compliance memo, or updates the tariff. Strata ends at a routed, cited recommendation.
  • Does not give legal advice. Materiality and impact judgements are decision support for the analyst, not a substitute for counsel's sign-off on anything that leaves the building.
  • Does not monitor external dockets on its own. Ingestion in this build is a version handed to the system, not a crawler watching commission websites for new filings. That is a real product gap for a production version, and it is out of scope for 48 hours.
  • Does not route to more than one kind of owner. This bullet used to say the product models a single reviewer role. That stopped being true during the build and the correction matters. Ten role templates now exist, each named for a function rather than a rank — a Director of Load Forecasting and a Director of System Operations approve different things, so "Director" is not a permission. Regulatory counsel and certifying officer are separate roles that approve, and the workspace administrator approves nothing by default, because the person who draws the route should not be the person it routes to. No template holds both action.propose and action.approve; a four-person team may legitimately want both, so they grant it deliberately rather than inheriting it from a default. The templates live in scripts/seed_roles.py, which nothing runs automatically — neither make seed nor the deploy entrypoint calls it, so a fresh database has none of them until somebody runs it. What is still true, and is the narrower claim worth defending: the one routing target the pipeline resolves is the obligation owner, a named person on the obligation record. The roles are who may approve, not a second user the product is designed for.
  • Does not build a multi-jurisdiction taxonomy. First on the cut list, and never started. Note the shape of this gap now: data/real/ holds filings from eight commissions, so the excuse of having no cross-jurisdiction data has expired while the work has not begun. Nothing in the code knows that "final order" arrives on a different clock in Ohio than in Utah.
  • Does not treat a paraphrase as a citation. A claim that cannot be traced to an exact, matching source span is not shown as fact, full stop — even if a human reader would find the paraphrase obviously correct. This is the trade-off named in ADR-003 and it is accepted deliberately: rejecting a good citation costs a review; accepting a bad one costs the product's reason to exist.

Trust and adoption — what would actually earn it

An expert user does not trust a compliance tool because it looks confident. The metrics below are what this build proposes to measure trust by. The eval work landed (docs/.ai/tasks.html, Phase 4), so two of the six below are now results and four are still definitions. The eval harness runs in under a second, calls no model and opens no socket; it prints 5 of 5 metrics pass. (make eval wraps it in an install and a seed, so the target itself touches the database.)

Read the counts, and notice there are no percentages. The harness refuses to print a rate below ten independent samples, and it derives the sample size itself rather than taking a caller's word for it (ADR-039). Today that rule suppresses every percentage on the scorecard, including one that would have read 100%. That is the rule working: 20 offsets verify, but 9 of them quote the same boilerplate sentence, so they are 6 subjects, not 20 samples. A product that argues nobody should assert a claim they cannot substantiate does not get to report a flattering rate off six items.

The honest summary: this build reports citation verification, corruption rejection and change detection as counts, because those are properties of code that exists and the harness forbids dressing them as rates. It cannot report extraction precision — the corpus has no negative labels — nor queue health under real load, nor whether an analyst's behaviour changes. Those need, respectively, better labels, live usage, and a user in the room. And the largest caveat is the one the scorecard prints itself: no model runs in the eval, so a green board says nothing about model behaviour. It is a regression gate on the deterministic spine, and a strict one. It is not evidence that the AI is any good.

Beyond utilities — why the wedge generalises, and where it does not

The core primitive is domain-agnostic: successive versions of a governing text, a registry of the reader's own obligations, a deterministic diff, a citation-verified materiality judgement, and a routed action. Nothing in that chain is specific to public-utility-commission dockets. The prep guide's adjacent-user list names the obvious next markets: an independent power producer or energy retailer facing the same commissions; a banking compliance analyst tracking rule changes from a prudential regulator; a pharma regulatory-affairs analyst tracking FDA guidance revisions. Each has the same shape — a body issues successive versions of binding text, a company holds obligations against it, and someone has to work out what changed and route it.

What does not generalise for free is the parsing layer. PUC dockets, SEC filings and FDA guidance documents are structured differently — different section conventions, different signals for draft-versus-final, different citation formats. The deterministic diff and the citation verifier are format-agnostic once text is segmented with stable offsets; the segmentation step itself needs a domain-specific adapter per source type.

Be precise about what this build has, because "one adapter" overstates it. There is no PDF adapter at all. Every file in data/real/ arrived as text somebody had already extracted, and the product stores one .txt per filing beside a provenance record. So the layer that would break first on a new domain is the layer this build has not written for any domain, including its own. What the corpus does buy is a harder test of the layer underneath. The text is dirty in the ways real filings are dirty: docket captions, line-numbered testimony, certificates of service, words hyphenated across a line break, and — in 9 of the 102 files, counted rather than remembered — a soft hyphen or a ligature. Normalisation is exercised against real damage rather than against a corpus built to suit it, which is how the soft-hyphen and form-feed defects were found. Anyone quoting a fuller breakdown should recount it rather than copy the figures from the commit that added the corpus; those were measured with definitions that are not written down, and a count whose method is unstated is the kind of number this document is trying to stop repeating. Going deep on one domain rather than wide on three is the same choice ADR-001 makes about the user, applied to the source material; it is not a claim that the one adapter exists.

User feedback — the honest state of it

As of this writing, zero interviews are complete. T1 in docs/.ai/tasks.html is closed as failed rather than left open: 45 messages went out over two days, four bounced, and nobody replied. The tracking table in docs/user-research.html is still three empty rows. This section states the hypotheses under test and what would falsify each, because that is what is actually true right now — not because it is the strongest possible answer. A submission that invented findings here would score worse on inspection than one that shows the interviews in flight and the reasoning that will update this document the moment they land.

What was built instead, and the line it must not cross. A synthetic persona was assembled from the 102 real filings — docs/synthetic-user.html — and an interview was rehearsed against it in docs/synthetic-interview.html. Both pages open by saying nobody said any of it, that it is not evidence, and that no line may be moved into this document as a finding. That warning is the load-bearing part. A convincing fake transcript is the one artefact that could do this submission real damage, and the rule this PRD follows is that the persona generates questions to go and check, never answers to rely on.

It earned its place by what it could not do. Asked to walk through last Tuesday, it had nothing — so that question survived to the real script, because a question a synthetic cannot answer is exactly the one worth a real person's twenty minutes. On H3 it was deliberately not asked anything: the persona was built by the same hand that built the product, so its agreement would be worthless and its disagreement would be theatre. The output was eight sharper questions, each asking for an artefact rather than an opinion — "open your sent mail, what was the last version comparison you sent anyone?" in place of "does anyone diff today?" On H4 it moved the needle slightly and settled nothing: the best evidence in the corpus that interpretation is expensive is an Ohio witness who twice declined to say how two stipulations differ, and he is Commission Staff, not this user.

One real conversation reported straight beats three invented ones. If none land at all, that is reported too, and the hypotheses above stand as the design's stated bets rather than confirmed findings.

Two of the four this document used to list have closed, and keeping a closed question open reads as badly as closing an open one. One caution about the two files disagreeing: that list marks some further questions settled on the strength of code that is not committed. Where it and this table disagree, this table describes the repository as it stands.

QuestionState
Which model, and what the fallback is when it is wrong or unavailableClosed by ADR-037. One module may call one model, claude-opus-5, pinned in code rather than read from the environment so a silent swap leaves a trace. Three named off states that announce themselves, not one that degrades quietly.
Whether audit history is an event log or snapshotsClosed by ADR-016, ADR-017 and ADR-031: an append-only hash-linked event log, with a correction written as a new row superseding the old. The rollback half is not closed — ADR-032 records that the reversal column exists and the digest scheme that would defend it does not, so nothing may treat a reversal as evidence. That is a gap, stated as one.
Where the confidence threshold sits, and on what evidenceOpen, and blocked behind the corpus rather than beside it. Settling it needs an eval set big enough to count the cost of a false escalation separately from the cost of a false assertion at several threshold values. The present corpus cannot do that, so the number stays unset rather than invented.
What the obligation-extraction eval set is, and who labels itOpen. Nobody has labelled one. The reason this cannot be quietly self-served: ground truth labelled by the person who built the system measures agreement with oneself, which is a sanity check wearing the clothes of an accuracy claim. It needs somebody who does the job.

None of these gets a placeholder answer. An invented number reads worse under questioning than an open item marked open, and this project has already found the cost of the alternative: several of the corrections in this document existed because a page kept describing something after the code beneath it moved.

The four above are the questions that bear on what this document promises. docs/.ai/decisions.html carries the full numbered list, including questions about scheduling and the ingestion boundary that were moving while this page was being reconciled. That list is the live one; this table is the subset a reader of the PRD needs, and it is deliberately not a mirror.
