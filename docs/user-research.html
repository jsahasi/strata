User Research

Who was spoken to, what they said, and what changed in the build because of it.

This is the highest-leverage hour of the 48. AI Fund's prep guide names it directly: "Get real user feedback on your prototype before you submit — this is the most useful thing you can do." The PRD is read specifically for how user feedback shaped the build, and the panel digs into it. A submission with a working prototype and no user conversations loses a whole evaluation dimension. Send the requests before writing code; run the conversations while the build is in flight.

Who to ask

Target: a regulatory affairs analyst or manager at a utility, an independent power producer, or an energy retailer. Adjacent and still useful: compliance analysts in banking or pharma who track rule changes; a regulatory lawyer, provided you discount their answers — their incentives differ from an in-house analyst's.

Where they are: LinkedIn (search "regulatory affairs" + utility names), former colleagues who moved into energy or compliance, anyone in the AI Fund and AES orbit met at the build lunch. Ask for twenty minutes, not an hour.

The request, short enough to answer from a phone

I'm building a prototype for turning regulatory change into tracked work — dockets and orders into "here is what changed, here is what it affects, here is who reviews it." I'd value 20 minutes to hear how you do this today and where it hurts. I'm not selling anything; I want to know if I'm solving a real problem or an imagined one.

What to ask — and what not to

Ask about the last time, not about preferences in general. "Walk me through the last docket you had to act on" produces facts; "would you use a tool that..." produces politeness.

AskWhat you are listening for
Walk me through the last regulatory change you had to act on. Start from how you heard about it.The trigger. Is it an alert, a colleague, a law firm memo, a docket subscription? This tells you what the product replaces.
How did you work out what actually changed between versions?Whether anyone truly diffs, or whether they read the new version cold. If they redline by hand, that is the wedge.
How do you decide whether it is material to you?The judgement the product must support rather than replace.
Who else had to look at it before anything happened?The real routing graph, versus the org chart.
What did you write down, and where does it live now?The system of record you must fit alongside. If it is a spreadsheet, say so in the PRD.
When were you last burned — something missed, or acted on too early?The pain with a dollar figure attached. This becomes the PRD's opening.
What would you need to see before you trusted a machine reading of a docket?Direct evidence for or against the citation-verification bet (ADR-003).

Do not describe your solution until the end. Once you do, they will be agreeable, and agreeable answers are worthless.

Then show the prototype and stay quiet

Ask them to do one task without narration from you. Record three things: what they misread, what they ignored, and what they asked for that is not there. Silence while they struggle is the data.

Hypotheses under test

H1The analyst, not counsel, is the person who does the change-to-action work.Two or more interviews say the real reading is done by outside counsel and the analyst only files it.open
H2Nobody reliably diffs versions today; it is done by reading, memory and highlighting.They show a redlining tool they already trust and use routinely.open
H3Trust requires seeing the source passage, not a confidence score.They say a score would be enough, or that they would not check either way.open
H4The expensive part is interpretation and routing, not detection.They say detection is the bottleneck and interpretation is quick once they know where to look.open

H3 is the one to watch. If trust does not require the source span, the depth area (ADR-003) is engineering for its own sake — and the honest response is to say so in the PRD and keep the verifier anyway as a correctness mechanism rather than a trust one.
