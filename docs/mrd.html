Market Requirements Document

The market, not the user. The PRD argues that one analyst has a real problem; this document argues that the problem sits inside a market big enough, urgent enough, and defensible enough to build a company on — and says plainly where that argument is not yet proven.

Status at time of writing. Zero user interviews have landed (docs/user-research.html — all three rows still read "—"). Every claim below about buyer behavior, willingness to pay, or felt cost is market reasoning from public structure, not something a regulatory-affairs person has confirmed. Where an interview would settle a claim, this document says so and names the hypothesis rather than smoothing over the gap.

The market, and who is in it

The category. Regulated enterprises — companies whose obligations change not because they choose to change them, but because a government body issues an order and the clock starts. That category is wide: utilities, banks, insurers, pharma manufacturers, telecoms, pipeline operators. Strata does not target the category. It targets one beachhead inside it, for the same reason ADR-001 names one user instead of a persona list: a product built for "regulated enterprises" is a product built for no one.

The beachhead. Investor-owned utilities and independent power producers. Both live under a regulatory structure with a specific shape: a company's obligations are set proceeding by proceeding, docket by docket, at one or more state public utility commissions and, for anything that touches wholesale power or interstate transmission, at FERC. A multi-state holding company does not run one open docket — it runs one per jurisdiction it operates in, each on its own procedural clock, each amended independently. That structure, not a market-size figure, is the argument for why this is hard: the unit of regulatory obligation is fragmented by design, and no single filing tells a company everything it now owes.

Why not name a market size. A defensible TAM number needs a source — utility count, average regulatory-affairs headcount, spend per docket — and none of that is sourced here. Rather than invent a figure to look rigorous, this document reasons about the shape of the market and marks the size itself as an open question the eventual go-to-market plan has to answer with real numbers, not this one with invented ones.

Why now

Three forces are moving at once, and they compound rather than add.

Sourcing note, to match the standard set above. The specific claims below — FERC Order No. 2023 and the interconnection backlog it addresses, and data-center-driven load growth — are asserted from industry knowledge, not sourced here. They are probably true and they matter to the argument. This document does not delete them for lack of a citation; it flags them, the same way the TAM section above flags the absence of a sourced market-size figure rather than inventing one.

AI-driven load growth. Large new electricity demand from data centers is pushing utilities to file for generation, transmission, and rate changes on a schedule utilities have not run at in decades. Every one of those filings opens or amends a docket. More dockets per utility means more proceedings a regulatory-affairs analyst has to track in parallel, each with its own version history.

Interconnection reform. FERC's interconnection rulemaking (Order No. 2023 and the state-level proceedings that followed it) rewrote how generation projects join the queue, specifically because the prior process built a backlog FERC's own rulemaking record describes as multi-year. Reform of that scale does not land as one final rule — it lands as a sequence of orders on rehearing, compliance filings, and state tariff amendments, each one a new version of a proceeding an IPP has to re-read against its existing project queue.

The pace of energy-transition rulemaking. State clean-energy targets, integrated resource planning cycles, and the state-level implementation of federal incentive programs are producing more proceedings, not fewer, and each proceeding revises more often before it settles. The volume of change is rising faster than regulatory-affairs headcount is rising to track it — that gap is the market opening, and it is a structural one, not a one-year spike.

The compounding effect. None of the three forces is new by itself. What is new is that they land on the same desk at the same time: more proceedings, more amendments per proceeding, and a workforce of analysts whose headcount has not scaled with either. A tool that only helps with one force helps less than the timing suggests. A tool that speeds up reading a proceeding regardless of which force generated it is the one that matters now.

Buyer versus user

The user (per ADR-001). The regulatory-affairs analyst who reads the docket, works out what changed, and tells the business what it must do. This is who the product is built for and who the PRD is written to.

The buyer. Almost certainly not the same person. The analyst rarely holds a software budget. The likely buyer is the person the analyst reports to — a VP of Regulatory Affairs, a chief compliance officer, or general counsel — someone who is measured on whether the company missed a deadline or acted on an order that later got vacated, not on how many hours the analyst spent reading. That is a hypothesis, not a finding: H1 in user-research.html asks a version of this question and no interview has yet confirmed or falsified it.

Why the difference matters, not just as sales trivia. The user is sold on time saved and trust — does the tool read the docket correctly, and can they verify a claim without redoing the work themselves. The buyer is sold on risk — can the company show, if a regulator or an auditor asks, that a specific order was seen, assessed, and acted on within the deadline, with a record of who reviewed it. Those are different pitches with different proof. A demo that only shows the analyst's daily win risks stalling at the buyer's desk; a pitch that only shows the audit trail risks a tool nobody in regulatory affairs actually opens.

Open. Whether the buyer is regulatory affairs' own budget or sits inside a broader compliance or legal software line is unconfirmed. It changes who gets pitched and what the tool gets compared against, and it is exactly the kind of question a real conversation answers in five minutes and speculation does not.

Incumbents and adjacent tools

Four categories already sit near this problem. Each does something real. None does the specific join Strata is built around: an exactly-cited change, in a specific proceeding, mapped to this company's own obligations and projects, with an action and a reviewer attached.

CategoryWhat it doesWhere it stops
Regulatory change management / GRC platforms Watch regulatory feeds and dockets, tag new filings by topic or jurisdiction, alert a subscriber when something posts, sometimes map a tag to an internal policy. The alert is the product. It does not diff the new version against the last one, does not distinguish a draft comment period from a final order at the data-model level, does not quote the exact passage that changed, and does not know this company's specific obligations or projects — only the topic tags an administrator configured. The interpretation work ADR-002 names as the expensive part starts after the alert, not before it.
Trade and supply-chain compliance suites Screen transactions against denied-party and sanctions lists, classify goods against tariff schedules, check a shipment or a counterparty against a fixed, structured ruleset. Built for a different shape of problem: a lookup against a ruleset that changes occasionally, not an evolving proceeding that changes on its own procedural clock and has to be read as prose, not matched as a code. The engine is optimized for structured lookups; a docket amendment is unstructured text with legal consequence, and that is the harder problem this category was never built to solve.
Legal research tools Search regulatory and case text, track citations, surface related filings and headnotes, support a lawyer building an argument or a memo. A search tool, used per query, not a maintained state. It has no notion of "this company's obligation register" or "this company's open projects" to map a result against, and nothing persists between searches unless the user builds that structure themselves outside the tool. It answers "what does the rule say" well; it does not answer "what does this mean for us" at all.
Law firms, as a service Read a proceeding, apply legal judgment, write a memo interpreting what changed and what it means, billed by the hour. The judgment is often good. The cadence is not: a memo takes days, and the per-hour cost means firms get engaged for the proceedings that already look material, not for the routine amendment that turns out to matter on closer reading. That selection effect is exactly where coverage gaps open — the amendment nobody thought worth a legal review is the one that gets missed, and no one finds out until it is binding.
Honest framing, not a named-competitor claim. The rows above describe what each category of tool does in general, not a specific vendor's current feature set — this document does not have sourced, current facts about any named product to state as fact, and states none. If a specific incumbent has already closed part of this gap, that is exactly the kind of thing a user interview or a competitive trial would surface, and it belongs in user-research.html and the ADR log, not guessed at here.

Where the wedge sits. Between the alert and the memo. The regulatory change management category tells you something moved; the law firm tells you, slowly and expensively, what it means. Strata's bet is the middle: an exactly-cited account of what changed, machine-mapped to this company's specific obligations and projects, fast enough to run on every proceeding amendment rather than only the ones that already look expensive — and honest enough, through confidence-gated escalation (ADR-006), to say when it does not know rather than guess.

Why this is defensible beyond being first

Not defensible. The model call itself. Any well-funded competitor can wire an LLM to a docket feed by next quarter. If the pitch for defensibility rests on having used AI first, it is not a pitch — every incumbent in the table above will add a model to their product this year regardless of what Strata does.

The floor, not the moat — the correctness engine. Citation verification against exact source offsets (ADR-003) and deterministic diffing before model interpretation (ADR-004) are necessary, not sufficient. They are table stakes: the work a buyer has to see before trusting the tool with anything else. A competent team rebuilds both in a few weeks — docs/future-enhancements.html says so plainly, and this document agrees rather than argues around it. These earn the right to be trusted. They do not keep a competitor out.

The moat — the obligation graph and the multi-jurisdiction taxonomy. The durable advantage is the mapping between how a regulator words a requirement and how a specific company words its own obligations, built proceeding by proceeding, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, for one specific company (ADR-002, T9). That mapping compounds per customer: a multi-state holding company's taxonomy gets more accurate and more entangled with its own operations the longer the tool runs, and it cannot be bought — a competitor starting today has to rebuild it customer by customer, not download it. The same logic makes best-practices.html's principle that "a derived corpus migrates all at once" true here: once the taxonomy is load-bearing, removing it is not a vendor swap.

Stated plainly: this moat does not exist yet. At launch there is no accumulated obligation graph and no multi-jurisdiction taxonomy — there is a working prototype against a synthetic corpus. The moat is a direction the product is pointed at, not an asset it holds today. It has to be earned proceeding by proceeding, customer by customer. Claiming it now, before a single customer's obligations have been mapped, would be the same overreach ADR-003 refuses to let the product itself make.

A further candidate — the audit trail becomes the record of record. If a compliance officer starts pointing to Strata's project state as the answer to "what did we know and when," removing the tool stops being a vendor swap and starts being a legal-risk decision. That lock is stronger than the floor, but slower to earn even than the taxonomy — it requires a customer to trust the tool with exactly the kind of record an auditor might ask for, and no 48-hour build can manufacture that trust.

Open, stated plainly. Nothing past the floor is proven. The working prototype demonstrates the floor — that the correctness engine works and can be shown working. It demonstrates nothing about the obligation graph, the taxonomy, or the audit trail, which need real usage over quarters, across real proceedings, to become true rather than merely plausible.

Expansion path, from one vertical outward

Step 0 — where the build stands. One user, one sector: the regulatory-affairs analyst at an investor-owned utility. This is deliberate narrowing per ADR-001, not the ceiling of the ambition.

Step 1 — same regulator archetype, adjacent players. Independent power producers and energy retailers sit under the same state-PUC-plus-FERC structure as utilities. The proceeding shape, the draft-versus-final distinction (ADR-005), and the citation engine transfer with little change; the obligation taxonomy shifts because an IPP's obligations run through interconnection agreements and power-purchase contracts more than through rate cases. Cheapest expansion: new obligation vocabulary, same engine.

Step 2 — adjacent regulator archetype. Telecom, regulated by the FCC and state utility commissions in a structurally similar docket-and-order pattern. The diff and citation engine still transfers; the obligation taxonomy and the judgment of what counts as material both need rebuilding, because "material" in a telecom tariff filing and "material" in a utility rate case are not the same question answered with the same evidence.

Step 3 — further out, and a real schema lift. Banking and insurance compliance, where rulemaking has a different shape entirely — rule-and-comment-period rather than docket-and-amendment — and pharma, where the trigger is often guidance rather than an order. The correctness engine is domain-general by design (ADR-003, ADR-004 do not reference energy anywhere in their logic); the extraction and materiality judgment are not, and treating them as if they transferred for free would repeat the exact mistake ADR-006 exists to prevent — presenting an untested judgment as settled.

The constraint that governs every step. Expansion is gated by repeating the discipline in user-research.html for the new vertical, not by copying the schema and assuming it holds. A second vertical earns nothing if the first one's hardest lesson — talk to the user before believing the taxonomy — gets skipped the second time because the roadmap wants to move fast.

What would have to be true for this to be a large business

The buyer needs a felt cost, not an inferred one. A VP of Regulatory Affairs or a general counsel has to be able to point to what a missed deadline or a wrongly-timed action actually cost the company — in a rate case, a penalty, or a compliance filing that had to be redone. Utility rate cases plausibly carry costs like this; whether the buyer actually reasons in those terms, or budgets against them, is unconfirmed and is exactly the kind of thing H1 and the "when were you last burned" interview question in user-research.html are built to surface.

The escalation queue has to stay usable at scale, not just in the demo. ADR-006's own trade-off names the risk directly: "a queue nobody empties is its own failure." The 48-hour build can show the queue working against a synthetic corpus of known size. A business depends on it staying fast enough to clear across many customers' real proceedings, which is an operating question the eval numbers in Phase 4 start to answer and do not finish answering.

Usage has to be continuous, not a one-time purchase. State PUC dockets run on an ongoing procedural calendar — proceedings do not end when a company buys the tool. If that reasoning holds, the product should see recurring, not one-time, use, which is what a subscription business needs. This is inference from how dockets work, not evidence from a customer, and should be labeled as such until a real account confirms it.

The engine has to prove general by actually going to a second vertical. Step 1 in the expansion path above is the test. If moving from utilities to IPPs takes a rebuild rather than a re-tag, the defensibility argument for candidate 1 above weakens considerably — the correctness engine would be general in theory but sector-specific in practice, and that gap only shows up by actually doing it.

The sale has to land inside an existing budget line, not compete with a hiring decision. Regulatory-affairs teams are more likely to get a tool budget than a net-new headcount request approved in the same cycle. A pitch that reads as "hire fewer analysts" invites resistance from the exact user the product depends on for adoption; a pitch that reads as "the analyst you have covers more ground" does not. This is a positioning judgment made now, without a buyer conversation to test it, and it should be checked against the first sales conversations the company has, not assumed permanent.

What is not yet true, stated without softening. No user interview has been conducted. No buyer has been spoken to at all — the interview plan targets analysts, not the budget holder above them. No eval numbers exist yet (Phase 4 of the build board). No pilot customer, named or anonymous, exists. A large business needs all four before it is a large business rather than a well-argued idea; this document is the argument, not the proof.
