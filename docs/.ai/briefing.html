Questions they have told us they ask

These are close to verbatim from the guide. Have an answer for each, in your own words, out loud, before the panel.

Technical

Why this retrieval method over that one?

Lexical first, semantic only where paraphrase matters. Regulatory text is quotation-heavy and citation-bearing, so exact terms carry meaning and lexical search is strong, debuggable and free. Company obligations are written in the company's own words and will never lexically match the docket's — that specific join is where embeddings earn their place. Concede without being asked: the corpus is far too small to demonstrate a retrieval difference, so this is accepted on reasoning, not measurement (ADR-008).

What happens when retrieval fails on some kind of query?

This is the strongest structural answer in the design, so do not rush it. Retrieval does not enumerate changes — the deterministic diff does. Retrieval only ranks context for interpretation. So a retrieval miss degrades explanation quality, it does not lose a change. That separation was the point of ADR-004. Then distinguish a retrieval miss from a retrieval service failure: a dead provider or corrupt index escalates to review rather than silently degrading, because a degraded impact mapping that announces nothing is the failure mode this whole design exists to prevent.

What is your fallback when the model is wrong?

The design assumes the model will be wrong and makes that survivable. A misquotation is caught by offset verification and never reaches the user as fact. A misjudged materiality is caught by confidence thresholds and escalates. What is not caught: a fluent, correctly-cited, wrong interpretation of a passage the model quoted accurately. Say that plainly — it is the honest limit, and naming it is worth more than pretending the verifier covers it.

How would you handle a 10x increase in latency?

The user-facing path is not the model path. Ingestion, diffing and verification are deterministic and fast; the model runs on interpretation, which is asynchronous work producing review items rather than a blocking request. So a 10x model latency lengthens the queue, it does not freeze the workspace. At that point the levers are batching interpretations per change set, caching by content hash so an unchanged passage is never re-interpreted, and degrading to diff-only output with everything escalated.

Where does modern AI fail here, and how did you design around it?

Three, and they are documented rather than improvised. Fluent misquotation — models produce plausible, subtly wrong quotes and prompting does not fix it; answered by verifying against source offsets. Silent degradation — a provider falls back or a signal becomes meaningless and everything downstream still returns confident output; answered by refusing to score a signal that cannot be computed. False precision — a 0.87 confidence implies calibration nobody has done; answered with coarse ordinal buckets, calibrated against eval outcomes rather than taken on faith.

Attack the citation verifier. Where does exact-offset matching break?

Lead with the one they may not find: wrong-occurrence citations. Regulatory documents repeat boilerplate, so a citation can carry exact offsets landing on occurrence A while the model reasoned about occurrence B. The verifier confirms the quote matches at those offsets and passes it — green, and wrong. The corpus contains that trap deliberately (nine occurrences of one sentence) so it can be demonstrated and fixed rather than merely described. Then the rest: tables lose row structure when flattened and a cell offset is not gradable without its header; footnotes have no anchor to the clause they qualify; PDF extraction introduces ligatures and column bleed that the synthetic corpus does not contain, so the golden-file tests do not cover the risk they appear to.

Product

Who is this for, specifically?

One sentence, no hedging: a regulatory affairs analyst at a multi-state investor-owned utility. Then their day — how change reaches them, what they do with two versions of a docket, who they must convince, and where the record of that work currently lives.

Why are alerts and search insufficient?

An alert says something moved; the analyst still opens both versions, finds the delta, judges materiality, decides whether it binds yet, and works out which of their own obligations it touches. Search is worse — it assumes they already know what to look for. The expensive part is interpretation, and neither tool does any of it.

What did users tell you, and what changed because of it?

The one you cannot fake. Whatever happens, answer with specifics: what they misread, what they ignored, what they asked for that was not there. If only one conversation lands, say so and report it honestly — one real conversation reported accurately beats three implied ones, and they can spot an unfilled template instantly.

What does this become in 12 weeks, and in six months?

Explicitly asked for. 12 weeks: live docket ingestion instead of uploaded files, and a second jurisdiction proving the taxonomy generalises. Six months: the obligation graph as the compounding asset — the mapping between how a regulator words a requirement and how a specific company words its own, which is per-customer, cannot be bought, and is the actual moat. The verifier and the diff are the floor, not the moat; say that before they say it to you.

Concede these before you are asked

Volunteering a limit converts it from a hole they found into discipline you showed. Each of these is true, and each will be found.

  • No model runs in the product. Concede this first, unprompted. grep -rn anthropic app/ returns nothing, and a reviewer will run it. What ships is the harness a model plugs into: claims are bound to (version, char_start, char_end, quoted_text), the binding is re-checked against the stored source before display, and a claim that fails is withheld rather than shown with a score. Today the claims come from deterministic assembly over a corpus with the traps built in — a sentence repeated three times per version so text equality alone is not enough, a restructure that scores 0.94 on raw similarity. The honest framing: I built the part that is hard to retrofit and easy to get wrong, and left the part any competent team can add in a week. If they push, agree that an untested integration is a real risk and say what would change: the model proposes claims, nothing else about the path moves.
  • "Verified" means the quote is real, not that the interpretation is right. Offset verification proves the words exist where claimed. It says nothing about whether the reading of them is correct.
  • Rollback does not exist. The audit history is hash-chained, append-only, and refuses UPDATE and DELETE at the session level. The undo half was never built. Say the word "rollback" yourself rather than letting them find the gap under a heading that promises it.
  • Reviewer routing is a shared queue, not routing. Everything withheld lands in one pile anyone can pick from. Escalation records who resolved an item but has no assignee, and there is no obligation table to route to — the owners exist only as names in data/company_context.json. This is the gap between the demo and the pitch, and the pitch sentence "routes a recommended action to the person who owns it" is the one to soften.
  • The eval set is small, self-labelled, and its headline number was inflated. The scorecard printed "20 of 20 offsets, 100%", but only 10 of those 20 are distinct strings and 9 are the same boilerplate sentence — so the harness applied its own no-rate-below-ten-samples rule everywhere except where applying it would suppress a 100%. Found by an adversarial pass, not by a failing test. Do not defend the number; describe the rule and how it was caught.
  • Retrieval is architecturally present, not demonstrated. The corpus is too small to show a difference.
  • Ordinal confidence is not inherently more trustworthy than a decimal. It is a coarser bucket that resists false precision, and it needs calibrating against eval outcomes.
  • The moat does not exist at launch. The verifier and diff are table stakes; the obligation graph has to be earned customer by customer.
  • Cold start is a real weakness of a change-centric product. One version of a proceeding produces nothing yet.

The 90-second pitch, written out

They ask for this directly. Adapt the words, keep the shape: who, what, why, what is hard. Time it — 90 seconds is roughly 220 words, which is shorter than it feels.

Regulatory affairs analysts at multi-state utilities live inside a problem nobody has solved for them. A commission publishes a revised order. Somewhere in eighty pages, three things changed that matter to them and forty that do not. They open both versions side by side and read, because that is genuinely the state of the art. Then they work out which of their own obligations each change touches, decide whether it binds yet or is still a proposal, and chase down the person who has to approve doing something about it. That takes days, and the cost of missing one is a penalty or a stranded project.

Strata turns a regulatory change into tracked work. It reads successive versions of a proceeding, computes exactly what changed, tells you whether it is draft or final, and attaches each change to the projects it affects. Routing it to the named owner of the obligation it lands on is the next build, not today's — today everything the system will not assert goes to one review queue.

The hard part is trust. An analyst cannot act on a machine reading of a docket unless they can see the exact words behind every claim. So every claim in Strata carries a citation into the source, and the system verifies that citation against the original text before it will show the claim as fact. If it does not verify, the claim does not get asserted — it goes to review. That constraint is the product.

Your hardest technical choice, explained to a non-expert

Also asked for directly. Practise this until no jargon survives — no offsets, no indices, no embeddings.

Language models are fluent liars about quotations. Ask one what a document says and it will give you a sentence that sounds exactly right, is mostly right, and has a word changed in the middle that reverses the meaning. You cannot prompt that away, and in this domain it is fatal — an analyst who cannot trust the quote has to go and read the source, which is the work we claimed to remove.

So Strata is built so that nothing gets to assert a quotation on its own say-so. When the system makes a claim, it records precisely where in the original document that claim came from — which document, which version, which stretch of text. Before showing the claim, it goes back to the original and checks that the words are actually there, exactly as quoted. If they are not, the claim never appears as established fact. It goes into a review queue with the reason. That check does not care whether a model or a script produced the claim, which is the point: I wrote the constraint first, so that connecting a model later cannot weaken it.

The cost is that we sometimes reject a quotation that was fine, because a stray line break or a curly quote made the check fail. That is a review someone did not need to do. I chose that direction deliberately: a rejected good citation costs a minute, and an accepted bad one costs the reason anyone would use the product.

Scripts for the hard moments

When you do not know

The guide says this reads as a positive signal. Do not reach — reaching is the thing that fails. The shape that works:

I have not worked with that. Here is how I would approach finding out — I would start by [concrete first step], because the thing I would need to know first is [the actual unknown].

What makes this strong is the second clause. Anyone can say "I don't know." Naming what you would need to learn, and why that is the thing that matters, shows you understand the shape of the problem even where you lack the answer.

When they find a real hole

They will. Some you have already listed to concede; some you will not have seen. Do not defend, and do not collapse either — both read badly. Take it, say what it costs, say what you would do:

That is a real gap, and I had not thought about [X]. What it would break is [specific consequence]. The fix is probably [direction], and the reason I would not have caught it in 48 hours is [honest reason]. Is that the failure you were pointing at, or is there a worse version of it?

The last question is the move. It converts being caught into working the problem with them, which is what the residency actually is.

When they ask about the energy domain

You are not an energy person, and AES will be in the room. Claiming domain depth is the one bluff certain to be caught.

I am not an energy person, and I would not pretend to be with AES sitting here. What I know is the shape of the problem — a regulated enterprise, external rules changing faster than anyone can read them, and a workflow where being confidently wrong is more expensive than being slow. The domain expertise is on your side of the table, and that is the right way round: I would rather learn the docket structure from someone who lives in it than tell them what their job is.

When they ask what the AI wrote

The defensible answer names decisions you made and proposals you rejected. Keep docs/submission.html current so this is recall, not reconstruction.

I decided the architecture and used Claude Code as an implementer. The decisions in the log are mine — splitting the deterministic diff from the model interpretation, verifying citations against source offsets, letting confidence escalate rather than guess. Where I rejected what it produced: [specific example]. The place it genuinely improved on my plan was [specific example], and I took that.

Have two real examples ready in each direction. Vague answers here are read as evasion.

When they push on "one user" versus the obligation owner

One user, one workflow. The analyst is who the product is designed for. The obligation owner exists because approval needs a second human — the person who interprets a change should not be the person who signs off acting on it. That is a control, not a second product. If I designed for both, I would satisfy neither.

When they ask why you, and why now

Founder-market fit is a named thread and it is not about the domain.

I spent 25 years building software people rely on, took one company from nothing to the NYSE, and shipped a generative-AI product line into an installed base last year. This year I have been building AI systems hands-on again, and the lessons that stuck were all about trust: a fallback that quietly returned plausible garbage for months while every check passed, a score nobody recomputed that went stale invisibly. That is the same failure this product exists to prevent, in a domain where it costs real money. I am drawn to it because I have paid for that mistake and I know how to design against it.

What you would change — have this list ready

"Defend the choices you made and acknowledge the ones you would change." Having no answer reads worse than having several. These are honest ones for this build:

WhatWhy you would change it
Derive every wiring guard from the application, never from a list a person maintainsTwo view modules — 2,000 lines and nine routes — were included nowhere while base.html linked to one of them from every page, so the nav on every screen pointed at a 404. The wiring test passed throughout, because it asserted five hand-written paths. A list a person maintains cannot catch a module that person forgot. The guards now derive from app.routes. The panel itself

A conversation with AI Fund partners, usually onsite, moving through three threads: your TDD, your PRD, and AI fluency plus founder-market fit. Clear communication is stated as part of the evaluation — speak slowly, and ask a panelist to rephrase rather than guessing at the question.

Role fit, stated in the guide: "Engineer in Residence is an engineering role. If your primary background is product or business rather than engineering, talk to your recruiter before submitting." Your background is genuinely both, and your recent work is hands-on code. Lead with the engineering — the 200,000 lines shipped this year, the systems you debugged this week — and let the operating history be the second thing they learn, not the first.
