Question No. 1 

Provide  an  update  on  the  status  of  negotiations  with  data  centers  and  other  large  load  projects 
seeking to develop sites in North Carolina.  

a.  Provide an overview of backup generation contemplated for these projects.  
b.  Please  state  whether  any  of  these  projects  are  anticipated  to  be  co-located  load  with 
generating facilities. If so, please describe the measures that will need to be implemented 
to ensure grid reliability and fair cost to customers. 

Response: 

Currently, all of the data centers the Company currently serves or is studying are located within 
the Company’s Virginia jurisdiction with a high concentration in “Data Center Alley” in Northern 
Virginia.  There  are  currently no active data  center development projects  in  its  North  Carolina 
jurisdiction.  In August 2024, Natron Energy announced the development of an approximately 65 
MW battery manufacturing facility in Edgecombe County, NC, representing the latest large load 
customer in the Company’s North Carolina jurisdiction.1 

As of July 2025, one data center in the Company’s Virginia service area is served by a co-located 
generating  facility,  owned  by  the  data  center  itself.  This  facility  and  the  associated  load  is  not 
connected to  the grid.  The Company is  not  aware of any active plans for additional co-located 
generating  facilities  in  its  service  territory.  In  addition,  all  data  centers  have  on-site  backup 
generation that can be used during grid emergencies or service interruptions.  The vast majority of 
the installed backup generation at data centers in the Virginia service territory is fueled by diesel, 
which, pursuant to EPA regulations2  are not able to be used as co-located generators to serve day-
to-day load requirements.

Question No. 2 

Describe potential cost allocation issues or other issues attributable to large load additions/data 
centers of which the Commission should be aware, including co-located loads. Provide examples 
from other jurisdictions. 

Response: 

The goal of any cost allocation methodology and rate design is to—as best as possible—ensure 
that each customer or customer class is paying their share of the utility’s system costs. 

Data centers as an industry are providing critical support for what has become an essential service 
in our society, as well as important economic development benefits to the localities where they 
locate.  As discussed in the Company’s response to Question 1, it has not experienced data center 
interest  in  its  North  Carolina  service  area  yet,  but  it  has  experienced  interest  from  large  load 
customers more generally. Properly managed, an expanded commercial and industrial customer 
base can benefit all utility customers. Energy intensive customers create costs for the system, but 
they also spread the utility’s fixed costs over more units of consumption and, for this reason, could 
help to lower system average costs borne by all customers over the long-term.  

To  date,  the  Company  believes  that  its  current  allocation  methodologies  have  appropriately 
addressed the goal of equitable allocation of costs to the various customer classes. The Company’s 
current  cost  allocation  methodologies  and  rate  designs  are  not  resulting  in  the  class  cross-
subsidization and the classes are within the zone of reasonableness in terms of their relevant rates 
of return of the system costs they are causing. 

Dominion does not have a separate rate class in North Carolina for high load factor customers, 
such as data centers; such customers would generally be included in the Company’s Large General 
Service classes (North Carolina Schedules 6L, 6C, 6VP, 6P, or NS ). In Virginia, such customers 
are generally included in the GS-3 and GS-4 classes. 

As new data centers have connected over the past several years in the Company’s Virginia service 
area, the GS-3 and GS-4 classes’ proportion of energy and demand has increased; and, as a result, 
the relative allocation of system costs to the GS-3 and GS-4 rate classes has naturally increased in 
turn.    With  the  growth  of  large  load  customers  in  Virginia,  however,  there  has  developed  a 
significant difference within the GS-4 customer class between the smaller GS-4 customers—who 
have peak demands just above 500 kW—and the large load customers who have peak demands of 
over  25  MW.  The  large  load  customers,  because  of  their  size,  require  the  Company  to  make 
significantly  larger  infrastructure  investments  in  order  to  provide  service  to  these  customers. 
Additionally, these customers also generally have a higher load factor, measured by the customer’s 
average demand divided by the customer’s peak demand. 

As the large load customers continue to grow and dominate the ranks of the GS-3 and GS-4 classes 
in Virginia, they create a meaningful impact on the results of the allocation factor calculations used 
to  allocate  costs  to  these  classes:  because  the  large  load  and  high  load  factor  customers  have 
different characteristics than other GS-3 and GS-4 customers, there is a potential for intra-class

generation  related  costs.    Dominion’s  North  Carolina  rate  schedules  do  not  include  similar

customers in this jurisdiction, then it may be appropriate, at that time, to consider the adoption of 
similar provisions in North Carolina.  The Company does not see a need for any changes to its 
North Carolina rate schedules at this time, however, as those rate schedules remain appropriate for 
the large load industrial customers we serve.

Question No. 3 

Describe  any  potential  modifications  to  the  North  Carolina  Interconnection  Procedures  (NCIP) 
that would be necessary to interconnect generation resources for large electric load customer/data 
centers and describe whether the current study process timeframe under the NCIP is sufficient for 
generation to serve large electric load customer/data centers. Provide examples of interconnection 
procedure revisions from other jurisdictions. 

Response: 

At this time the Company has not identified any modifications to the NCIP that would be needed 
to interconnect generation resources for large electric load customers, including data centers, in 
North  Carolina.  PJM  Interconnection,  LLC’s  (“PJM”)  queue  reform  is  currently  underway  to 
transition  from  a  serial  queue  to  a  cluster  study  process,  and  PJM  is  not  accepting  new 
Interconnection Requests until 2026. These revisions are, however, anticipated to relieve some of 
the bottleneck challenges in PJM’s interconnection queue and help generators interconnect more 
efficiently. Aside from generally improving the timing component of the interconnection process, 
the Company does not anticipate the PJM interconnection reforms to have a material impact on 
the interconnection of generation resources for large electric load customers specifically.

Response to Question Nos. 4-7: 

Mitigating against the risk of stranded costs is a  forward-looking exercise. This process begins 
with robust forecasting of customer needs. Forecasting electric load involves determining future 
electricity demand based on various factors such as historical usage patterns, weather conditions, 
economic trends, population growth, contractual agreements, and customer intelligence. Accurate 
load forecasting is essential for ensuring a reliable power supply, optimizing grid operations, and 
reducing  costs.  Long-term  forecasts  support  infrastructure  planning  and  generation  investment 
decisions. 

In the Company’s service territory, data centers are driving a significant amount of load growth.  
Dominion’s ability to forecast data center load growth is distinct in that Dominion serves more 
data centers today than the next five domestic markets combined. Dominion has more than eleven 
years of monthly data center metered information, which it combines with customer intelligence 
and  market  research  to  develop  load  predictions.  Those  predictions  are  then  validated  against 
signed contracts and customer requests. 

The Company’s current data center forecasting methodology and contracting process has proven 
effective at accurately forecasting demand growth such that Dominion is confident the load growth 
projected for the Company over the next 10-15 years is real and is coming.

Dominion  is  keenly  aware  of  the  risks  associated  with  inaccurate  forecasting  to  the  utility, 
consumers,  and  the  overall  power  grid.  Overestimating  demand  may  result  in  unnecessary 
generation, leading to higher operational costs and potentially increased environmental impacts.  
Conversely,  underestimating  demand  can  cause  supply  shortages,  impacting  customer  growth 
plans, broader economic development activities, and grid reliability.  For this reason, the Company 
forecasts data center load separately from  all other load to yield the most reliable and accurate 
overall load forecast. 

With  this  unprecedented  load  growth,  substantial  and  significant  investment  across  generation, 
transmission, and distribution will be needed. Dominion is committed to ensuring that customers 
driving  additional  investments  pay  a  fair  share  of  those  investments  and  continue  to  bear  cost 
responsibility in the event of a change in market conditions, regulations, or technology. Indeed, 
long-term investments require long-term commitments, beyond what is currently afforded by the 
Company’s approved terms and conditions for utility service. Prior to filing the Virginia biennial 
rate  case,  Dominion  carefully  examined  its  contracting  provisions  to  determine  where 
enhancements were appropriate.   

Ultimately,  the  Company  determined  that  within  its  Virginia  jurisdiction,  updated  terms  and 
conditions for electric service are needed to account for the large and growing volume of large 
load customers in Dominion’s Virginia service area.  As noted above, this proposal is currently 
pending before the Virginia State Corporation Commission in the Company’s biennial rate case 
proceeding,  Case  No.  PUR-2025-00058.  Key  tenants  of  that  proposal,  which  also  includes  the 
expansion of customer classes discussed earlier in these comments, are displayed below.

Question No. 8 

Describe any value of, and best practices for, tariffs allowing large-load customers to self-supply. 
Provide examples from other jurisdictions. 

Response: 

Large-load  customers  are  interconnecting  across  the  country  into  regions  with  a  diverse  set  of 
interconnection  and  facility  design  options,  including  front  of  the  meter  and  behind  the  meter 
configurations. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) has yet to establish a set 
of standards or best practices for large-load customers. However, FERC has pending proceedings 
that may result in rules defining best practices and self-supply in tariffs. FERC issued a show cause 
order to explore potential rules to establish the process for co-locating large-load customers with 
generation.8 Additionally, FERC recently held a technical conference to examine the challenges 
of resource adequacy in the RTO and ISO regions where participants contemplated the need for 
large-load customers to self-supply.9
