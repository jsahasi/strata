1

A. 
The AEP Ohio Stipulation, consistent with the Application, applies the new tariff only to

11

12 
the Data Center Stipulation, which applies financial obligations to both data centers and 
13 
traditional manufacturing and other large loads.  As I describe in my testimony opposing

15 
development and puts Ohio at a competitive disadvantage when it comes to attracting 
16 
large manufacturing plants and other large job creators.  The data-center-only approach of 
17 
the AEP Ohio Stipulation avoids those negative effects on economic development that 
18 
will result from the “shotgun” approach adopted in the Data Center Stipulation.

Q. 
How does the AEP Ohio Stipulation benefit ratepayers and the public interest by 
1 
balancing the risk of underutilized investment between data centers and other 
2

3 
A. 
As I explained in my testimony opposing the Data Center Stipulation, a key question for 
4 
the Commission in this case is who should take on the risk of data centers not “showing 
5 
up” or using the amount of power they say they will use.  That is, who will pay for any 
6 
underutilized transmission infrastructure that will be built to serve data centers – data 
7 
centers or other customers?   
8 
 
 
AEP Ohio filed its Application in this proceeding to mitigate the risk that 
9 
transmission infrastructure will be built for speculative data center projects, and when it 
10 
comes time to serve, the data center projects either will be cancelled or be using 
11 
significantly less power than they had planned.  If this happens, more of the costs of the 
12 
investments built to serve data centers will be shifted to other customers.  One key 
13 
question for the Commission, therefore, is how to allocate this risk of underutilization 
14 
among data centers and AEP Ohio’s remaining customers. 
15 
It is important to realize, moreover, that allocating this risk of underutilization is a 
16 
zero-sum game between data centers and AEP Ohio’s other customers.  When data 
17 
centers take on stronger financial commitments based on their higher power needs (for 
18 
example, higher minimum demand charges and stronger collateral requirements), there is 
19 
less risk that the costs of underutilized transmission infrastructure will be shifted to AEP 
20 
Ohio’s remaining customers.  The opposite is true when data centers take on weaker 
21 
financial commitments. 
22 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 81 of 330
9 
 
 
The AEP Ohio Stipulation benefits customers and the public interest by striking a 
1 
reasonable balance between these competing interests.  Several provisions of the AEP 
2 
Ohio Stipulation – including provisions addressing contract term (¶ III.B), collateral

requirements (¶ III.C), minimum demand charges (¶ III.D), the load ramp period (¶ III.E),

7

the input of data centers that the terms as proposed in the Application were too harsh or 
9 
difficult for them to plan for, including lower minimum demands charges (¶ III.D), a 
10

more certainty before they sign Electric Service Agreements (“ESAs”), including 
12

Q. 
How does the AEP Ohio Stipulation benefit ratepayers and the public interest by 
15 
establishing appropriate incentives for data center customers to accurately estimate 
16 
their power needs? 
17 
A. 
The provisions in the AEP Ohio Stipulation that create additional financial commitments 
18 
from data centers establish strong incentives for data centers to estimate their true power 
19 
needs up front (i.e., when they sign an ESA).  This means that the data center customer’s 
20 
load will be accurately reflected in AEP Ohio’s load forecast, which is provided to PJM 
21 
to be used in the PJM transmission planning process.  Therefore, whatever EHV 
22 
transmission assets or other investments that result from the PJM transmission planning 
23 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 82 of 330
10 
process will be driven by accurate, right-sized load forecasts to meet AEP Ohio’s actual 
1 
load. 
2 
 
 
A main problem with AEP Ohio’s existing tariff structure (and the Data Center 
3 
Stipulation) is that is it encourages data centers to overestimate their load and sign up for 
4 
more power than they need.  When customers sign up for a certain amount of contract 
5 
capacity in an ESA, AEP Ohio must ensure that this full amount of contract capacity is 
6 
available to serve the customer if the customers uses it.  Yet the current tariff’s 60% 
7 
minimum demand provision provides a 40% “buffer” – essentially a free capacity option 
8 
that data centers can sign up for and that AEP Ohio must make available, but data centers 
9 
need not pay for.  Instead, it is AEP Ohio’s other customers who will fund the 
10

same problem arises with the Data Center Stipulation, whose 75% minimum demand for 
12 
the largest loads allows for a free 25% capacity option paid for by AEP Ohio’s other 
13 
customers.  For very large data center loads, this free option funded by other customers 
14 
can represent hundreds of megawatts, equivalent to the output of a small power plant.  
15 
For instance, under the Data Center Tariff, a data center who needs only 750 MW can 
16 
sign up for 1000 MW of capacity without paying anything for the free capacity option of 
17 
250 MW.  In the context of AEP Ohio’s 30,000 MW Central Ohio data center queue, the 
18 
amount of free “buffer” capacity can increase drastically – equivalent to multiple power 
19 
plants – even if only a portion of that queue signs ESAs.   
20 
 
 
The AEP Ohio Stipulation addresses this issue by imposing an 85% minimum 
21 
demand (¶ III.D) and a longer contract term (¶ III.B) and backing these provisions with 
22 
appropriate collateral (¶ III.C) and exit fee (¶ III.J) provisions.  These provisions strike 
23 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 83 of 330
11 
the right balance of establishing meaningful, impactful incentives for data centers to 
1 
accurately estimate their own load, while also providing some flexibility and leeway if 
2 
the data centers’ estimates are inaccurate.

Q. 
How does AEP Ohio Stipulation Paragraph III.A.6 regarding the BTCR programs 
7 
benefit ratepayers and the public interest? 
8 
A. 
Allowing data centers to participate in the 1CP and 6CP BTCR programs would seriously 
9 
undermine the financial incentives created by the minimum demand provisions in the 
10 
Data Center Tariff, and therefore prevent data center participation in these programs is 
11 
vital to fulfilling the goal of the Data Center Tariff.   
12 
For most customers, charges under the BTCR are determined by the higher of (a) 
13 
the customer’s actual peak demand each month or (b) the minimum billing demand set by 
14 
the tariff.  Under the 1CP and 6CP BTCR programs, participating customers’ BTCR 
15 
charges are set based on the customer’s usage during the coincident peaks on the 
16 
transmission system, either the single (1CP) or six (6CP) highest coincident peaks.  In 
17 
other words, the customers’ minimum billing demand (e.g., 60% of contract capacity for 
18 
Schedule GS) is effectively overridden by the customer’s 1CP or 6CP usage.   
19 
Accordingly, if Schedule DCT customers were allowed to participate in the 1CP 
20 
or 6CP BTCR programs, the minimum demand provisions in Paragraph III.D – i.e., the 
21 
minimum demand levels of 60-85% depending on the size of the data center – would be 
22 
replaced by the customer’s usage during the 1CP and 6CP.  For example, under the AEP 
23 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 88 of 330
16 
Ohio Stipulation, a cryptocurrency mine with a 300 MW contract capacity would have 
1 
minimum monthly demand charges based on 255 MW.  Cryptocurrency mines can and do 
2 
voluntarily reduce their load when energy prices are high.  If the cryptocurrency mine in 
3 
our example is allowed to participate in the 1CP or 6CP BTCR programs and is able to 
4 
reduce its load to, for example, 10 MW during the 1CP or 6CP, then its monthly demand 
5

Q. 
What are the main distinctions between future data center customers and other 
1 
large loads that justify applying Schedule DCT only to new data centers? 
2 
A. 
There are at least three reasonable distinctions that justify applying different tariff 
3 
schedules to new data centers and other large loads. 
4 
First, data centers, unlike other large loads, have been responsible for the 
5 
significant growth in AEP Ohio’s service territory in recent years and are expected to 
6 
drive substantial future growth.  AEP Ohio's peak demand in Central Ohio was 
7 
approximately 4,000 MW in 2018.  This means that since AEP Ohio was founded in the 
8 
early 20th century, it took about 100 years for the load in central Ohio to grow to 4,000 
9 
MW.  Yet it will take only 10 years from 2018 to 2028 for data centers to more than 
10 
double that load, with an additional 5,000 MW in data center load expected in Central 
11 
Ohio by 2028 due to signed ESAs.  No other industry in AEP Ohio’s long history has 
12 
caused such rapid growth in such a short time frame.  This unique hypergrowth feature of 
13 
data center load alone is sufficient for the Commission to conclude that data centers are 
14 
unique in their use of electricity. 
15 
Second, data centers are unique compared to other large loads in the sheer amount 
16 
and concentration of electric demand at one location.  Other than data centers, the largest 
17 
customers in AEP Ohio’s service territory are a few steel mills that use approximately 
18 
100 to 150 MW of electric demand in a single location.  In the past, AEP Ohio’s largest 
19 
customer was an aluminum smelter with a peak demand of approximately 400 MW, 
20 
which was unique.  In contrast, data centers have requested 1,000 MW or more in a single 
21 
location, posing unprecedented risks regarding the continued viability of the data center 
22 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 110 of 330
38 
company, its location, and its product.  AEP Ohio must construct significant infrastructure 
1 
to serve such concentrated loads, making data centers particularly risky. 
2 
Third, and perhaps most importantly, data centers bring with them systematic, 
3 
industry-wide risks.  AEP Ohio’s typical non-data-center growth consists of a diverse 
4 
array of customers from various industries.  This diversity provides inherent protection 
5 
against building assets that may go unused due to downturns in a particular industry or 
6 
segment of the economy.  For example, if the automotive market experiences a downturn, 
7 
automobile parts manufacturers may use less electricity, but at the same time, another 
8 
industry such as agricultural processors might be thriving and use more electricity.  In 
9 
this way, the incremental ups and downs of different industries are mitigated, and there is 
10 
a diminished risk of cost shifting to AEP Ohio’s other customers from trouble in a single 
11 
industry.  With data centers, by contrast, AEP Ohio is facing the prospect of over half of 
12 
its Central Ohio load (or more) all being part of the same data center industry.  The 
13 
unprecedented scale and high-stakes nature of this risk facing AEP Ohio and its 
14 
customers means that data centers represent a large, systemic risk concentrated in a single 
15 
industry.  Increasingly, data centers’ vast demand for power is driven by speculate 
16 
investments that are betting that AI technology will be transformative and highly 
17 
lucrative.  If the data center industry experiences a downturn in five to ten years, or if use 
18 
cases for artificial intelligence do not materialize, or if data centers improve their 
19 
computing efficiency and need less power, these systemic risks could lead to significant 
20 
underutilization of the electric infrastructure that was built to power the data centers.  
21 
These risks, moreover, cannot be mitigated by the marginal ups and downs of other 
22 
industries in AEP Ohio’s service territory, since the data centers will constitute such a 
23 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 111 of 330
39 
large percentage of AEP Ohio’s load.  Given the fact that data centers present uniquely 
1 
systematic and concentrated risks associated with hyperscale data center loads, it is 
2 
appropriate to require additional financial protections from the data center industry before 
3 
substantial transmission investments is built to meet their projected electric needs.  In my 
4 
opinion, data center customers are not similarly situated to other customer classes, and 
5 
the hyperscale, hypergrowth nature of their load sets them apart from other customer 
6 
classes.  Accordingly, their respective terms and conditions of service need not (and 
7 
should not) be the same.
