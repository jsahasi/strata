23

Q. 
What is the regulatory principle of cost causation? 
10 
A. 
The principle of cost causation is that those who cause a cost to be incurred 
11 
should pay for such costs. The principle does not require perfect apportionment of 
12 
all costs to the cost causer. Practically speaking, perfect cost causation is rarely 
13

14

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 
1 
 
2 
54. 
Q. 
Can you briefly summarize why the 10/23 Stipulation should be approved 
3 
and the 10/10 Stipulation should be rejected? 
4 
A. 
Yes. As I explained above, Staff has been guided in this case by a desire to 
5 
improve reliability, certainty, and accountability, all for the benefit of ratepayers, 
6 
in addressing the challenges of new data center load. As is often the case in 
7 
Commission proceedings, competing interests must be balanced. The 10/23 
8 
Stipulation is a reasonable compromise that balances the interests of data centers 
9 
and non-data-center customers. Data centers want to locate in Ohio on terms and 
10 
conditions that are fair and reasonable to them. Non-data-center customers want 
11 
protection and assurances that their bills will not become unaffordable because 
12 
they are paying for new infrastructure that is built to accommodate data center 
13 
projects. 
14 
 
15 
The 10/23 Stipulation provides a substantially better balance of interests than the 
16 
10/10 Stipulation: 
17 
• 
By proposing a tariff that applies only to data centers, the 10/23 
18 
Stipulation seeks a data-center-specific solution to an issue being driven 
19 
by data centers. The 10/10 Stipulation, in contrast, casts a wider net, 
20 
subjecting Ohio manufacturers and other non-data-center industrial 
21 
customers to a new tariff and its new obligations. 
22 
AEP Ohio Supp. Page 192 of 330
 
59 
• 
The 10/23 Stipulation includes long-term contracts, coupled with a three-
1 
year exit fee. The 10/10 Stipulation, in contrast, includes long-term 
2 
contracts with either a one-year exit fee or no exit fee at all. 
3 
• 
The 10/23 Stipulation increases minimum demand charges for data center 
4 
customers, with the largest data centers paying a minimum demand 
5 
charge of 85%. To borrow a phrase from AEP Ohio, this gives data 
6 
centers appropriate “skin in the game.” In contrast, many large data 
7 
centers under the 10/10 Stipulation could pay a minimum demand charge 
8 
of just 75%, which shifts too much risk to non-data-center customers. 
9 
• 
Under the 10/10 Stipulation, every data center under 50 MW would be 
10 
exempt from the new tariff. This shifts too much risk to non-data-center 
11 
ratepayers and creates substantial uncertainty in load forecasting. The 
12 
10/23 Stipulation’s 25 MW threshold is more reasonable. 
13 
• 
The 10/10 Stipulation has a massive loophole allowing data centers to 
14 
participate in AEP Ohio’s transmission pilots, which could allow data 
15 
centers to avoid the minimum demand charges that are essential to protect 
16 
other ratepayers. 
17 
• 
The higher load ramp minimums under the 10/23 Stipulation (50%, 65%, 
18 
80%, 90%) compared to the 10/10 Stipulation (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%) 
19 
more reasonably assign costs to the cost causers: data centers. 
20 
 
21 
When viewed as a package, the 10/10 Stipulation is skewed toward the interests 
22 
of data centers. It allows them to avoid financial responsibility for the costs that 
23

60 
they cause and shifts far too much risk to non-data-center customers. The 10/23 
1 
Stipulation, in contrast, tends to the needs of both data centers and non-data-
2 
center customers alike. It should be approved without modification because it 
3 
passes the Commission’s three-part test and is superior to the 10/10 Stipulation.
