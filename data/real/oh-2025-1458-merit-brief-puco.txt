INTRODUCTION 
This case arises from the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio’s (Commission or PUCO) 
consideration of a novel and increasingly consequential development in the state’s electric-utility 
landscape: the rapid growth of large-scale data centers seeking service. Faced with projected 
demand from customers in Ohio Power Company d/b/a AEP Ohio’s (AEP Ohio) territory whose 
energy usage far exceeds that of traditional commercial or industrial consumers, the Commission 
was asked to determine whether a new tariff was necessary to address the operational and cost 
implications of that growth.  
AEP Ohio filed an application proposing a tariff applicable to new data center and mobile 
data center customers designed to accommodate the potential for unprecedented load growth, 
driven almost exclusively by data centers locating within its service area. At issue below was 
whether a special tariff was needed to address the unique needs of data centers. The Commission 
made the correct factual determination that data centers are different from other large loads and 
that a distinct tariff for data centers is appropriate to address the unique load characteristics, cost 
allocation, and infrastructure concerns presented by the unprecedented scale and nature of data 
center demand. 
In the Order now under review, the Commission resolved several contested legal and 
procedural issues, executing its responsibilities fairly and in full accordance with Ohio’s public-
utility administrative law. The Commission determined that the application did not constitute an 
increase to existing rates but instead proposed a tariff applicable to a distinct class of new 
2 
customers. It allowed a temporary moratorium on certain data center contracts to continue during 
the pendency of the proceedings, until the effective date of the new tariff. The Commission 
upheld the Administrative Law Judges’ (ALJ) rulings on challenges concerning notice and 
service and sustained the ALJs’ evidentiary rulings made during the presentation of testimony. 
And the Commission made detailed findings of fact grounded in extensive evidence, all within 
the scope of its statutory authority and regulatory discretion. 
The Commission’s decision, concluding that data centers pose unique, systemic risks that 
do not exist for other large energy intensive customers, and adopting the stipulation and tariff 
accounting for that, was reasonable and supported by the evidence and should be affirmed.

STANDARD OF REVIEW 
R.C. 4903.13 provides that a final order made by the Public Utilities Commission shall be 
reversed, vacated, or modified by the Supreme Court on appeal only when, upon consideration of 
the record, the Court is of the opinion that such order was unlawful or unreasonable. The Court 
does not “reweigh the evidence or second-guess the PUCO on questions of fact.” Lycourt-
Donovan v. Columbia Gas of Ohio, Inc., 2017-Ohio-7566, ¶ 35. The Court “will not reverse or 
modify a commission decision as to questions of fact when the record contains sufficient 
probative evidence to show that the commission’s decision was not manifestly against the weight 
of the evidence and was not so clearly unsupported by the record as to show misapprehension, 
mistake, or willful disregard of duty.” In re Application of Ohio Power Co., 2018-Ohio-4698, ¶ 9 
(citing Monongahela Power Co. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2004-Ohio-6896, ¶ 29). “The application 
of the relevant legal standards to the facts is one that is best left to the PUCO in the first 
instance.” Complaint of Wingo v. Nationwide Energy Partners, L.L.C., 2020-Ohio-5583, ¶ 26.  
This Court has “consistently refused to substitute [its] judgment for that of the 
commission on evidentiary matters.” AK Steel Corp. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2002-Ohio-1735, ¶ 13; 
citing, Cincinnati Gas & Electric Co. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 86 Ohio St.3d 53 (1999); also citing, 
Dayton Power & Light Co. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 4 Ohio St.3d 91 (1983); also citing, Columbus v. 
Pub. Util. Comm., 170 Ohio St. 105 (1959). “The burden is on the appellant to show that the 
commission’s decision is against the manifest weight of the evidence or is clearly unsupported by 
the evidence.” Id. at ¶ 20. “This burden is difficult to sustain because the court has consistently 
found it proper to defer to the commission’s judgment in matters that require the commission to 
5 
apply its specialized expertise and discretion … with regard to factual matters now on appeal.” 
Monongahela Power Co. at ¶ 29. Further, this Court will not reverse a Commission order absent 
a showing by an appellant that it has been or will be harmed or prejudiced by the order. Myers v. 
Pub. Util. Comm., 1992-Ohio-135, ¶ 18. 
As to questions of law, this Court has “complete and independent power of review” in 
appeals from the Commission. Ohio Edison Co. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 78 Ohio St. 3d, 466, 469 
(1997). Even so, this does not prevent the Court from drawing upon the specialized expertise of 
the Commission in interpreting the law when “dealing with highly specialized issues and where 
agency expertise would, therefore, be of assistance in discerning the presumed intent of our 
General Assembly.” Office of Consumers’ Counsel v. Pub. Util. Comm., 58 Ohio St.2d 108, 110 
(1979). While the Court does not give deference to administrative agencies in matters of 
statutory interpretation, this case does not involve the construction of ambiguous statutes. 
TWISM Ents., L.L.C. v. State Bd. of Registration for Prof’l Eng’rs & Surveyors, 2022-Ohio-
4677. Moreover, the Court has recognized that an agency has considerable discretion regarding 
the implementation of a statute within its jurisdiction. The agency’s exercise of its authority need 
only fall within a zone of reasonableness. In re Application of Firelands Wind L.L.C., 2023-Ohio-
2555, ¶ 15. 
The record contains sufficient probative evidence demonstrating that the Commission’s 
decisions were lawful, reasonable and supported by the manifest weight of the evidence. The 
Commission properly determined that data centers are fundamentally different from other large 
load customers, and for that reason, Schedule DCT is a just, reasonable, and lawful pathway for 
data center customers to receive service from AEP Ohio while ensuring that other customers are 
6 
protected from the potential financial risks associated with the massive influx of data center load 
to the Ohio grid.  
ARGUMENT 
Proposition of Law 1: The Commission lawfully and reasonably approved Schedule DCT, 
which is based on actual, material differences in service and is neither unduly 
discriminatory nor unlawful. 
 
In its First Proposition of Law, Appellant argues that the Commission’s decision was 
against the manifest weight of the evidence and discriminated against data centers in violation of 
R.C. 4905.32, 4905.33, 4905.35, due process, and the Equal Protection Clause. Appellant also 
briefly argues under its proposition that the Commission’s decision was not based upon sufficient 
findings supported by the record in violation of R.C. 4903.09. The Court should reject 
Appellant’s arguments because the treatment of data center customers under Schedule DCT is 
reasonably based on unique risks posed by data center load and the Commission’s Opinion and 
Order and Entry on Rehearing contained ample detail on the factual basis and reasoning 
supporting the Commission’s decision. 
R.C. 4905.32 directs that no public utility shall “refund or remit directly or indirectly, any 
rate … or charge except such as are specified in such schedule and regularly and uniformly 
extended to all persons … under like circumstances for like, or substantially similar, service.” 
R.C. 4905.33 prohibits utilities from charging different rates for customers who receive “like and 
contemporaneous service under substantially the same circumstances and conditions.” R.C. 
A. 
Schedule DCT is consistent with R.C. 4905.32, 4905.33, and 4905.35 because 
different customer classes may be established based on justifiable and 
reasonable distinctions, and those statutes do not prohibit discrimination 
when the classes are reasonably based. 
7 
4905.35 prohibits utilities from providing service in a manner that would create “any undue or 
unreasonable prejudice or disadvantage.”4 
Interpreting these statutes, this Court has consistently held that a utility tariff is 
considered unduly discriminatory only if it treats similarly situated customers differently without 
justification. Allnet Communications Serv., Inc. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 70 Ohio St.3d 202, 207 
(1994) (holding that under R.C. 4905.32 and R.C. 4905.35(A), “discrimination is not prohibited 
per se but is prohibited only if without a reasonable basis”); See Weiss v. Pub. Util. Comm., 
2000-Ohio-5 at ¶ 11 (holding that R.C. 4905.33 prohibits discriminatory pricing for “like and 
contemporaneous service” rendered “under substantially the same circumstances and 
conditions,” but does not prohibit differences in prices charged or collected where the utility 
services rendered are different or if they are rendered under different circumstances or 
condition). Thus, these statutes do not prohibit all discrimination in service and rates; only 
“undue or unreasonable” discrimination is prohibited. See Weiss, 2000-Ohio-5 at ¶ 11; Allnet, 70 
Ohio St.3d at 207. 
 In Mahoning County v. Pub. Util. Comm., 58 Ohio St. 2d 40, 44 (1979), this Court 
confirmed:

Absolute uniformity in rates or prices is not required by statute or case law. A 
reasonable differential or inequality of rates may occur where such differential is 
based upon some actual and measurable differences in the furnishing of services to 
the consumer. 
 
Ohio law thus allows differentiation between customers and customer groups. Customers can be 
treated differently if there is a reasonable basis for the differing treatment. Allnet, 70 Ohio St.3d 
at 207.  “Differences reasonably affecting the expense or difficulty of performing the same or 
similar service in different areas or circumstances” may be reflected in cost recovery 
variations/differences, which would not be deemed unlawful nor discriminatory. Migden-
Ostrander v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2004-Ohio-3924 ¶ 311 (citing Buckeye Lake Chamber of 
Commerce v. Pub. Util. Comm., 161 Ohio St. 306, 312 (1954)) 
The record establishes that the Court’s well-established holdings in these cases are 
applicable to the Commission’s Order here. See, e.g., Allnet, 70 Ohio St.3d at 207. Here, the 
Commission properly found that the facts established on the record indicate reasonable 
distinctions between data center customers and other large-load customers. Order at ¶¶ 124-128, 
Appellant’s App. at 217-219.  
Appellant OMAEG aptly summarizes the collective effect of R.C. 4905.32, R.C. 4905.33 
and 4905.35: “In other words, similar customers must be treated similarly and charged like rates 
for utility services.” (Emphasis in original.) Appellant Brief at 11. But Appellant’s summary 
highlights why its argument fails: data centers are not similar to other large load customers—as 
demonstrated by extensive record evidence of their differences—and thus, there is no legal 
requirement that they be subject to the same tariff terms and conditions as other large-load 
customers, like traditional manufacturers. 
The distinctions that justify Schedule DCT are well-established in the record. Data center 
customers present differences that impact AEP Ohio’s difficulty of delivering service to data 
9 
center customers compared to other large energy-intensive customers. Order at ¶ 129, 
Appellant’s App. at 220. The Commission properly determined that AEP Ohio “has proven that 
there is a real, pending transmission constraint concern that stems from data centers’ significant 
load growth, and that the 10/23 Stipulation is a proactive solution to address these challenges.” 
Order at ¶ 121, Appellant’s App. at 215. The Commission's Order specifically identifies and 
discusses key distinctions of data centers that differ from all other Ohio electric customers, each 
supported by evidence in the record. Order at ¶¶ 122, 152, Appellant’s App. at 215, 233. Data 
centers bring unique challenges to the electricity network in Ohio, including unprecedented 
hypergrowth, extreme load concentration, unique systemic risks, and demonstrated cost 
causation. 
The Commission’s Order explained that it is not just the volume and highly intensive 
pattern of electric usage that distinguishes data center customers from other large energy 
customers, like manufacturers. Order at ¶ 129, Appellant’s App. at 220-221. Testimony 
established that individual data centers are expected to be the largest customers in AEP Ohio’s 
service territory. Additionally, data centers are materially and distinctly different from other large 
customers in various ways, including their impact on the grid in the quantity used, the purpose 
for which used, and the duration of use. Id. 
Data centers do, in fact, have load profiles that differ from other large customers. Data 
centers can run at or near maximum capacity all day and every day, unlike traditional 
manufacturers. Id. citing OEG Ex. 1 at 7. Not only are data centers larger than other customers 
like traditional manufacturers, but they have the ability to scale up their operations and increase 
their load more readily than many other industries. Id. The size and scalability of individual data 
centers form a reasonable basis to distinguish them from other large load customers. Id. 
10 
Moreover, the data center industry is a riskier proposition than other large load customers 
operating in more established industries—data centers are more mobile than other industries and 
can more easily change locations and thus necessitate a more tailored solution to prevent non-
cost causing customers from paying to overbuild AEP Ohio’s transmission system. Id., citing 
Staff Ex. 1 at 17, Direct Testimony of Christopher Healey, Nov. 26, 2024, Appellee App. at 17.  
The record establishes that data center load is experiencing unique and unprecedented 
hypergrowth, unseen for other types of large loads. AEP Ohio witness Ali testified that there is 
contracted new load of existing and additional customers totaling 4,400 MW in the central Ohio 
areas by 2030, of which only 400 MW of use is from non-data center customers, which 
represents only eight percent of the growth. Order at ¶ 94, Appellant’s App. at 197; Order at ¶ 
122, citing AEP Ohio Ex. 2 at 3-6; Appellant’s App. at 215-216. Testimony established that there 
were over 30,000 MW of unsigned data center load requests looking to connect to the 
transmission system in the greater Columbus area. Id.  
Based on a series of studies run by the AEP Transmission Planning organization, the 
Commission found that even a fraction of the anticipated 30,000 MW load would result in 
numerous overload and voltage violations throughout central Ohio. Id. This data center 
hypergrowth is the predominant cause of projected transmission capacity constraints that would 
require costly transmission investment.  
In addition, the Commission found that data centers pose unique, systemic risks that do 
not exist for other large-scale customers. Order at ¶ 108, Appellant’s App. at 206-207. As the 
proportion of AEP Ohio's load dedicated to data centers increases, the risk of significant 
underutilization of infrastructure built to serve data center load increases exponentially. Id. 
Unlike non-data center load, which consists of a diverse array of customers from various 
11 
industries where risks can be mitigated by marginal fluctuations across different sectors, data 
center risk is concentrated and industry specific. Id.  
The Commission properly recognized that AEP Ohio’s transmission infrastructure is at a 
tipping point, where significant infrastructure investment is needed to meet demand in excess of 
approximately 10,000 MW in central Ohio. Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 45, Appellant’s App. at 256. 
The Company has already committed to 5,000 MW of new data center load, bringing the system 
dangerously close to this threshold. Id. Thus, there is a timely need for a proactive solution to 
address potential reliability issues in the AEP Ohio central Ohio region, which is specifically 
driven by data centers. Id. Documented in testimony and exhibits, these concerns create actual, 
material differences in the risk posed by data centers. The evidence also established that data 
centers, not other classes of customers, are driving the costly transmission upgrades. Order at

Evidence established that: (a) data center load is projected to grow by around 5,000 MW

(c) the transmission constraints requiring investment are driven by the data center growth. 
Neither Appellant nor the nearly 19 other intervening parties in this case presented any evidence

Appellant argues that AEP Ohio should have provided more detailed cost-of-service 
studies or additional quantified data to support adoption of Schedule DCT. Appellant’s Brief at

AEP Ohio initiated this proceeding to address the concern that this unparalleled load

address the risk of stranded investments necessary to support future data center load growth 
12 
before those costs were fully incurred. Order at ¶ 122, Appellant’s App. at 216. The Order

and takes steps to protect existing customers. Order at ¶ 131, Appellant’s App. at 223. 
As the Commission explained in its Entry on Rehearing, there is a timely need for a 
proactive solution to address potential reliability issues in the AEP Ohio central Ohio region, 
which is specifically driven by data centers. Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 45, Appellant’s App. at 256. 
At this stage of load growth expansion for AEP Ohio, detailed cost-specific information is not 
yet available, and the specific costs to serve individual data centers will vary on a case-by-case 
basis depending on their location, timing, and specific service needs. Id. Schedule DCT accounts 
for this fact by including detailed provisions to ensure that specific rates applicable to each 
customer are sufficient to cover the Company’s cost of service. Id. This approach is both 
reasonable and necessary given the forward-looking nature of the proceeding. 
Without Schedule DCT, transmission owners might make massive transmission 
investments to serve projected data center load, with the risk that if data centers fail to 
materialize or underutilize the infrastructure, AEP Ohio’s existing customers would bear the cost 
of stranded investments. Order at ¶¶ 121-129, Appellant’s App. at 215-221. The DCT Schedule 
addresses this risk by ensuring that data center customers bear appropriate responsibility for the 
infrastructure investments their load requires. Id. 
This cost causation principle is fundamental to utility regulation. Customers who drive 
the need for infrastructure investment should bear the costs of that investment. The record 
establishes that data centers are overwhelmingly the drivers of projected transmission investment 
in central Ohio. Requiring them to assume appropriate financial responsibility for that 
investment is equitable cost allocation.

Proposition of Law No. 2: The Commission lawfully and reasonably found that AEP Ohio’s 
temporary moratorium was reasonable and necessary and declined to impose penalties. 
In its second proposition of law, Appellant contends that AEP Ohio's temporary 
moratorium on new data center connections violated R.C. 4933.83 and R.C. 4905.22 and that the 
Commission erred in failing to find AEP Ohio in violation of these statutes and failing to impose 
18 
penalties on AEP Ohio for those violations. Appellant’s argument again fails on multiple 
grounds. It fails because AEP Ohio’s temporary moratorium was a reasonable, necessary, and 
lawful response to unprecedented data center load growth that threatened the reliability and 
adequacy of service to all customers and did not violate R.C. 4933.83 or 4905.22. It also fails 
because Appellant has not suffered any particularized harm.  
R.C. 4905.22 requires utilities to provide service that is “necessary and adequate” and “in 
all respects just and reasonable.” Similarly, R.C. 4933.83(B) requires electric utilities to furnish 
adequate facilities to meet the “reasonable needs” of consumers within their certified territory 
and to provide “physically adequate service.” 
These statutes do not impose an unlimited obligation to connect any and all loads 
regardless of system conditions. The Commission recognized that the obligation to serve is 
bound by reasonableness and physical feasibility which includes not only service but the ability 
to provide enough electricity to meet customer load. In re Application of Ohio Power Co. for 
Approval of a Special Contract Arrangement with Ormet Primary Aluminum Corp., PUCO No. 
96-999-EL-AEC, et al., 2006 Ohio PUC LEXIS 359, Opinion and Order *11 (June 14, 2006). If 
a utility cannot extend service without jeopardizing reliability or risking voltage collapse and 
blackouts, the obligation to serve does not compel immediate, insufficiently planned system 
expansion. 
In the Ormet case, the Commission found that where a utility could not take on a 
customer’s load without risking the ability to provide reliable and sufficient service to other 
customers, the utility was not able to provide physically adequate service. Id. at *11. The 
circumstances here are analogous. AEP Ohio faced a situation where committing to serve the full 
extent of requested data center load would jeopardize reliable service to existing customers due 
19 
to transmission system constraints. Under Ormet, AEP Ohio was not required—and arguably was 
prohibited—from making commitments that would compromise its ability to provide physically 
adequate service. Here, the issue is that AEP could not safely serve all the data center load that 
was expressing interest in Central Ohio. AEP Ohio has a duty to provide safe and reliable 
service. The utility took this reasonable step, then filed the data center case with the 
Commission. Now with the Commission’s Order, the moratorium is over and AEP has a clear 
process. AEP is not trying to deny any data center from connecting to the grid. 
The record contains substantial evidence that the moratorium was a temporary, justified 
and proactive measure to protect system reliability. AEP Ohio witness Ali, the transmission 
planning expert in the case, provided detailed testimony regarding transmission system 
constraints and the risks posed by unprecedented data center load growth. Mr. Ali testified that 
AEP Transmission Planning studies performed in the summer of 2023 indicated that adding more 
load without significant reinforcement to the grid could cause a voltage collapse, resulting in 
brownouts and blackouts for customers. Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 45, Appellant’s App. at 256-257 
citing Tr. Vol. I at 149-151, 265; Tr. Vol. III at 581, Appellant’s App. at 265. 
Mr. Ali explained that the Central Ohio transmission network faces a tipping point where 
significant infrastructure investment is needed to meet demand in excess of approximately 
10,000 MW. Order at ¶ 122, Appellant’s App. at 215. The Company has already signed 
agreements to add 5,000 MW of new data center load by 2030, bringing the system dangerously 
close to this threshold. Id., citing AEP Ex. 2 at 3-6. 
If AEP Ohio had committed to serving the full extent of requested data center load 
without transmission system reinforcement, it could have jeopardized its ability to provide 
reliable and sufficient service to existing customers. Whereas, if AEP Ohio only committed to 
20 
serving a portion of the requested data center load, it would not have been providing physically 
adequate service to those customers. Order at ¶ 113, Appellant’s App. 210. The Company instead 
implemented a temporary moratorium, notified affected customers, and initiated a Commission 
proceeding to develop a long-term solution. The Commission found that this temporary measure 
was implemented “to mitigate the Company’s load from escalating too quickly.” Order at ¶ 130, 
Appellant’s App. at 221.  
Moreover, upon the effective date of Schedule DCT, the Commission directed AEP Ohio 
to cease the moratorium and to process the queue of customer service requests. Order at ¶ 130, 
Appellant’s App. at 221-222. Therefore, with the lifting of the temporary moratorium, coupled 
with the implementation of 10/23 Stipulation’s provisions, the Commission properly concluded 
that “it should be abundantly apparent that the State of Ohio and its EDUs are open and willing 
to invest in their transmission infrastructure to serve the unique load data centers pose, while also 
instilling into these customers that they must make a commitment to bear a fair share of the cost 
of such buildouts.” Id. 
Since the Commission concluded that the moratorium was reasonable and did not find 
AEP Ohio in violation of R.C. 4905.22 or 4933.83, the Commission reasonably declined to 
impose penalties. Penalties are discretionary and appropriate only where a violation of law is 
established. R.C. 4905.54. There was no violation of law and, therefore, no imposition of a 
penalty.  
Imposing penalties for a temporary action undertaken to prevent voltage collapse and 
protect system reliability would have also directly undermined Ohio’s express state policy to 
“[e]nsure the availability to consumers of adequate, reliable, safe, efficient, nondiscriminatory, 
and reasonably priced retail electric service.” R.C. 4928.02(A). AEP Ohio’s proactive approach 
21 
here served the interests of both data center customers and existing customers. Penalizing this 
approach would discourage utilities from bringing novel issues to the Commission for resolution. 
The Commission reasonably concluded that penalizing AEP Ohio for acting to protect grid 
reliability would not serve the public interest. Entry on Rehearing ¶ 48, Appellant’s App. at 258-
259. Faced with an unprecedented influx of load requests from data center customers, AEP Ohio 
had to strike a balance between providing reliable and adequate service to existing consumers 
and the duty to make service available to new consumers. Order at ¶ 130, Appellant’s App. at 
221, Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 51, Appellant’s App. at 261. 
The Commission correctly supported AEP Ohio’s temporary moratorium. However, even 
if the Commission had erred, Appellant’s argument would fail because Appellant has not shown 
that it suffered any particularized harm. The Court has recently reiterated that a “party seeking 
reversal of a Commission order cannot meet its burden of establishing prejudicial error without 
furnishing an argument or citing evidence showing that it has suffered a particularized harm.” In 
re the Application of Ohio Power Co., for Authority to Establish a Std. Service Offer Pursuant to 
R.C. 4928.143, in the Form of an Elec. Security Plan, Slip Opinion No. 2025-Ohio-3034, ¶ 16-
18, citing In re Complaint of Buckeye Energy Brokers, Inc. v. Palmer Energy Co., 2014-Ohio-
1532, ¶¶ 23-24. Generalized claims of harm are not enough. Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 51, 
Appellant’s App. at 261. At no point has Appellant demonstrated that it or any of its members 
attempted to connect to the grid and were prevented from doing so by the moratorium, which 
applied only to data centers and only to Central Ohio. Further, the Commission’s Order explicitly 
ended the moratorium and approved a clear and transparent process for new data centers to 
obtain service in AEP Ohio’s service territory. Order at ¶¶ 130, 158, Appellant’s App. at 221-222, 
22 
237. Where, as here, Appellant’s merit brief fails to demonstrate that it was harmed by the 
moratorium imposed, there is no prejudicial error. 
Proposition of Law No. 3: The Commission lawfully and reasonably found that AEP Ohio 
demonstrated a real, pending transmission capacity constraint supported by substantial, 
competent evidence. 
Appellant contends that the Commission erred by finding that AEP Ohio demonstrated an 
actual transmission capacity constraint without sufficient evidence in the record, in violation of 
R.C. 4903.09. However, the record does contain substantial, competent evidence, including 
expert testimony, transmission planning study summaries, and documented load growth 
projections, that establishes that AEP Ohio faced a real transmission capacity constraint driven 
by unprecedented data center load growth.  
As discussed above, R.C. 4903.09 requires that the Commission file “findings of fact and 
written opinions setting forth the reasons prompting the decisions arrived at, based upon said 
findings of fact.” R.C. 4903.09. The Commission’s Order thoroughly evaluated this evidence, 
provided detailed findings supported by specific record citations, and complied fully with R.C. 
4903.09. Appellant’s disagreement with the Commission’s weighing of the evidence does not 
constitute legal error. 
In its Order, the Commission expressly found that AEP Ohio proved a real, pending 
transmission constraint concern stemming from significant and unprecedented data center load 
growth. Order at ¶ 129, Appellant’s App. 220-221. The Commission found that the central Ohio 
grid approaches a tipping point near 10,000 MW of load, that AEP Ohio has already signed 
approximately 5,000 MW of new data center load by 2030, that existing peak demand in central 
Ohio is approximately 4,000 MW, and that inquiries for an additional 30,000 MW of load have 
been received. Order at ¶¶ 121–122, Appellant’s App. at 215-216. The Commission found AEP 
23 
Ohio witness Ali to be credible when he testified that the Company more than doubled its 
projected future demand in the central Ohio region, from approximately 4,000 MW to 9,000 
MW, driven by new, large data center requests. Order at ¶ 122, Appellant’s App. 215-216, citing 
AEP Ohio Ex. 2 at 3-6. The Commission relied on AEP Ex. 2, (load projections and signed 
agreements), Sidecat Ex. 10, Appellant’s App. at 797-798 (summary of transmission planning 
studies), and live testimony from AEP Ohio witness Ali subjected to extensive cross-examination 
over multiple hearing days.  
AEP Ohio witness Ali, the transmission planning expert in the case, provided detailed 
testimony regarding transmission system constraints and the risks posed by unprecedented data 
center load growth. Mr. Ali testified that the existing peak demand in Central Ohio is 4,000 MW; 
that AEP Ohio has signed Electric Service Agreements and Letters of Agreement for new load 
with existing and additional customers to add 5,000 MW of load to the Central Ohio region by 
2030; that AEP Ohio has received over 30,000 MW of load requests from data centers that have 
not yet signed agreements but are seeking to connect to the transmission system in Central Ohio; 
and that the current Central Ohio transmission system cannot support load in excess of a total of 
10,000 MW before needing significant infrastructure enhancements. Order at ¶ 122, Appellant’s 
App. 215-216, citing AEP Ex. 2 at 3–5, Tr. Vol. I at 117, Appellant’s App. at 344. 
This testimony demonstrates that with 4,000 MW of existing peak demand and 5,000 
MW of signed new load, the system will reach 9,000 MW by 2030, which will approach the 
10,000 MW threshold that triggers the need for significant infrastructure investment. Order at ¶ 
152, Appellant’s App. at 233-234. 
Witness Ali’s testimony was supported by transmission planning studies performed by the 
AEP Transmission Planning organization. These studies evaluated three different load levels: (1) 
24 
the 5,000 MW of signed load that has already gone through the PJM process with all violations 
addressed; (2) load growth of 750 MW beyond what the system can currently support; and (3) 
load growth of 1,500 MW beyond what the system can currently support. Sidecat Ex. 10,

Proposition of Law No. 4: The Commission lawfully and reasonably concluded that AEP 
Ohio’s application was not for an increase in rates. 
In its Proposition of Law No. 4, Appellant argues that the Commission erred by treating 
AEP Ohio’s Application as an application to establish new rates rather than an application for an 
increase in rates. Appellant makes this distinction because an application for increase in rates 
triggers procedural requirements that Appellant argues (in Proposition of Law No. 5) were not 
met. The Court should reject both propositions of law because the Commission correctly 
determined that AEP Ohio’s Application was an application to establish new rates, not an 
26 
application for an increase in existing rates under R.C. 4909.18 and R.C. 4909.19. The 
Application did not seek to increase any rate charged to any existing customer. Instead, it 
proposed entirely new tariff schedules, applicable prospectively to newly defined data center 
customer classes, while expressly grandfathering all existing customers. 
R.C. 4909.18 distinguishes between (1) applications to establish a rate, classification, 
charge, or regulation, and (2) applications to increase an existing rate. The statutory trigger for 
heightened procedural requirements is an “increase” of an existing rate. As this Court has held, 
an application constitutes a rate increase only when it seeks “an increase of an existing rate.” 
Ohio Consumers’ Counsel v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2006-Ohio-5789, ¶ 18. Here, no existing rate has 
increased. 
The Commission found, “The Company’s application did not propose an increase in rates 
pursuant to R.C. 4909.18 … [and] does not involve a request to increase rates for existing 
customers because the proposed tariff involves the emergence of a prominent, new type of 
customer.” Order at ¶ 25, Appellant’s App. at 148. Further, the Commission noted that there 
would likely be no impact on existing customer rates due to grandfathering policies. Id. Data 
center customers that had signed service agreements prior to the effective date of the new tariffs 
were expressly grandfathered and would continue to be served under existing terms.  
The Commission explained that “the Company’s proposal should not impact customer 
rates, but rather, require heightened commitments prior to interconnection and service." Id. This 
is not a rate increase; instead, it is a prospective framework for a new customer classification. 
Schedule DCT contains explicit grandfathering provisions, and the Commission noted that there 
would likely be no impact on existing customer rates due to grandfathering policies. Order at ¶ 
25, Appellant’s App. at 148, 408. Customers with executed service agreements prior to the 
27 
effective date would continue under existing Schedule GS and would not be subject to any of the 
terms of Schedule DCT. No current customer’s authorized tariff rate was modified, amended, or 
increased. Any potential migration of grandfathered customers would occur only in a future base 
rate case. Order at ¶ 46, Appellant’s App. at 158. 
An increase presupposes a baseline. Prospective customers requesting new, specialized 
service have no existing rate to increase. Establishing initial service terms for a newly defined 
customer class is not an “increase” within the plain meaning of R.C. 4909.18. The Commission 
has treated similar filings as "not for increase" where existing customers are grandfathered. See, 
e.g., Cincinnati Gas & Electric Co., PUCO No. 82-622-EL-ATA (May 12, 1982) (new lighting 
service with higher rates not considered an increase because existing customers retained their 
prior service terms). 
In sum, AEP Ohio did not seek to increase any existing rate. It proposed new schedules 
for a newly defined customer class, with express grandfathering of existing customers. Under the 
plain language of R.C. 4909.18 and precedent, that is not an application for an increase in 
existing rates. Appellant’s fourth proposition of law should be rejected and the Commission’s 
Order affirmed. 
Proposition of Law No. 5: The Commission lawfully and reasonably upheld the ALJs’ 
decision regarding statutory notice requirements under R.C. 4909.18. 
Appellant argues that the Commission erred by proceeding to hearing without fulfilling 
the statutory notice requirements of R.C. 4909.18. As discussed above, the Court should reject 
Appellant’s argument because the Commission correctly determined that the application was to 
establish new rates and R.C. 4909.18 did not apply. Therefore, the notice and procedural 
requirements of R.C. 4909.18 and 4909.19 were not triggered. Even if those provisions had been 
triggered, the ALJS substantially complied with those requirements, so that no error occurred. 
28 
R.C. 4909.19 establishes two distinct circumstances that trigger mandatory notice and 
hearing requirements: (1) when an application seeks an increase in rates or charges, or (2) when 
the Commission, upon investigation, determines that an application may be unjust or 
unreasonable. But the statute does not mandate publication of legal notice for all applications or 
all hearings. The Commission has discretion to schedule hearings for other purposes, and when it 
does so, the notice requirements found in R.C. 4909.19 do not apply. 
Neither of the circumstances that trigger notice and hearing requirements under R.C. 
4909.19 occurred in this case. As discussed under Proposition of Law 4, the Commission 
expressly determined that AEP Ohio’s application did not constitute a request for an increase in 
existing rates. Order ¶ 25, Appellant’s App. at 148. The Commission also made no preliminary 
finding that the application “may be unjust or unreasonable.” Since neither circumstance 
occurred, the notice requirements of R.C. 4909.19 are inapplicable to this case. 
The Court should not conclude that the procedural requirements of R.C. 4909.19 apply 
simply because the Commission chose to hold a hearing. The matter was set for hearing in the 
Commission’s administrative discretion for this novel proposed tariff in the state of Ohio, given 
its significance and public interest, not because of any requirement in R.C. 4909.19. Entry on 
Rehearing at ¶ 58, Appellant’s App. at 265; Tr. Vol. I at 37, Appellant’s App. at 343. The ALJS 
correctly recognized that the Commission possesses discretion to hold a hearing even where R.C. 
4909.18 does not mandate publication. Id. The Court has repeatedly recognized the 
Commission’s broad authority to regulate its proceedings and manage its docket. See, e.g., Weiss 
v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2000-Ohio-5, ¶ 24; Duff v. Pub. Util. Comm., 56 Ohio St.2d 367, 374 
(1978). Consistent with that authority, the Commission has exercised its discretion to hold 
hearings in applications not involving rate increases without requiring statutory publication. 
29 
Reversal is also unwarranted because, even though the notice requirements of R.C. 
4909.19 did not apply, the Commission substantially complied with the statute. R.C. 4905.09 
provides that substantial compliance with Chapter 4909 is sufficient and that Commission orders 
“shall not be declared inoperative, illegal, or void for an omission of a technical nature.” The 
purpose of the notice requirements was fully achieved in this case. 
The purpose of notice, under R.C. 4909.18 and 4909.19, is to inform interested parties of 
pending proceedings and provide an opportunity to participate. This proceeding received 
extensive media coverage from its filing in May 2024. It was widely publicized, attracted 
intervening parties representing diverse interests, and generated substantial public comment. 
Order at ¶ 25, Appellant’s App. at 148. As the Commission observed, the proceeding “is one of 
first impression for this Commission and the state of Ohio,” and “all procedural steps taken in 
this case have been made publicly, in addition to being covered by several media outlets.” Id. 
Appellant has failed to name any party that has suffered injury because it was unaware of the 
proceedings or unable to participate. 
All procedural steps taken in this case have been made publicly, in addition to being 
covered by several media outlets, and public interest in the case has been robust, exhibited by the 
number of filed public comments and intervening parties. Order at ¶ 25, Appellant’s App. at 148. 
Moreover, upon consideration of the notice concerns raised, the Commission directed AEP Ohio 
to publish notice of a local public hearing. The evidentiary hearing commenced on December 3, 
2024 and continued for four days with full participation from all intervenors. Order at ¶ 14, 
Appellant’s App. at 146. During an extended continuance of the evidentiary hearing, the 
Commission scheduled a local public hearing for January 3, 2025 and directed AEP Ohio to 
publish notice of both that public hearing and the date when the evidentiary hearing would 
30 
reconvene. Order at ¶ ¶ 15-18, Appellant’s App. at 146. The hearing then continued on January 6, 
2025, again with full participation from a long list of intervenors. 
Even if the Court were to find any technical deficiency in the notice procedures, the 
supplemental notice provided during the continuance of the evidentiary hearing fully remedied 
any concern. Under these circumstances, the Commission substantially complied with the notice 
requirements of R.C. 4909.18, and Appellant has not demonstrated any prejudice. For these 
reasons, Appellant’s fifth proposition of law should be rejected and the Commission’s Order 
affirmed. 
Proposition of Law No. 6: The Commission lawfully and reasonably upheld the ALJs’ 
reasonable limitations on cross-examination. 
In its final Proposition of Law, Appellant contends that the ALJs improperly curtailed 
cross-examination of AEP Ohio witnesses Ali and McKenzie in violation of due process and 
R.C. 4903.09. A review of the record, however, shows that the ALJs exercised appropriate 
procedural discretion under Adm.Code 4901-1-27(B) to prevent cumulative, irrelevant, or 
improper questioning. The Commission carefully reviewed and affirmed those rulings and found 
no prejudice.  
The Commission did not deprive Appellant of its due process rights. Appellant had 
notice. A full hearing was conducted. Appellant called witnesses and cross-examined other 
witnesses. Appellant had the ability to file exhibits, file briefs, and file an application for 
rehearing. Under these same circumstances, this Court has previously held that due process was 
afforded. See, Vectren Energy Delivery of Ohio, Inc. v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2007-Ohio-1386, ¶ 53 
(finding that the party had notice, a full hearing, the ability to call its own witnesses, an 
opportunity to cross-examine other witnesses, the ability to file exhibits, and an opportunity to 
file briefs and an application for rehearing, and thus, it had due process). 
31 
Appellant was not denied due process because of evidentiary rulings made during cross-
examination. R.C. 4901.13 provides that the “[C]ommission may adopt and publish rules to 
govern its proceedings and to regulate the mode and manner of all . . . hearings relating to parties 
before it.” Weiss v. Pub. Util. Comm., 2000-Ohio-5, ¶ 11. R.C. 4901.13 gives the Commission 
broad discretion in the conduct of its hearings. Duff, 56 Ohio St. 2d at 379 . The Commission has 
the “discretion to decide how, in light of its internal organization and docket considerations, it 
may best proceed to manage and expedite the orderly flow of its business, avoid undue delay and 
eliminate unnecessary duplication of effort.” Toledo Coalition for Safe Energy v. Pub. Util. 
Comm., 69 Ohio St. 2d 559, 560, (1982).  
Consistent with this authority, the Commission has adopted rules that allow its ALJs to 
take such actions as are necessary to prevent the presentation of irrelevant or cumulative 
evidence. Adm.Code 4901-1-25 (C); Adm.Code 4901-1-27 (B)(7)(b). Adm.Code 4901-1-27(B) 
grants ALJs broad authority to govern the course of proceedings, exclude cumulative or 
irrelevant evidence, enforce evidentiary standards, and ensure orderly and efficient 
administration.  
Appellant argues that its counsel was limited in questioning AEP Ohio witness, Mr. Ali, 
regarding his modeling generation resources. However, the record demonstrates that Appellant 
was permitted to question Mr. Ali regarding the assumptions underlying his modeling. Appellant 
was permitted to ask whether additional generation resources were considered, whether PJM-
controlled resources were included, whether alternative technologies could mitigate constraints, 
and whether alternatives to the proposed 765 kV line were evaluated. Id; Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 
59-60, Appellant’s App. at 266. The ALJs limited repetitive, project-specific questioning 
regarding prospective generation resources after those topics had already been explored. Order at 
32 
¶ 31, Appellant’s App. at 151. The Commission properly found that the ALJs appropriately 
stopped repetitive questioning in the interest of administrative efficiency and clarity of the 
record. Order at ¶ 28, Appellant’s App. at 149-150. Properly limiting the scope of cross -
examination and preventing cumulative questioning does not violate due process. 
Appellant also argues that its counsel was not able to fully cross examine AEP Ohio 
witness Mr. McKenzie. The limitation during Mr. McKenzie’s cross examination involved a 
document containing pre-2019 data for which no proper foundation was laid and which witness 
McKenzie testified he had never seen before and could not authenticate. Order at ¶ 31, 
Appellant’s App. at 151. The ALJs appropriately prevented counsel from reading the document 
into the record without authentication while permitting questioning regarding its substance. The 
record further reflects that Appellant proceeded to question Mr. McKenzie extensively regarding 
economic development, data center growth, transmission capacity, and system support for new 
load. Order at ¶ 31, Appellant’s App. at 151. Based on this record, the Commission appropriately 
concluded that the ALJs placed “reasonable parameters” on questioning and that Appellant was 
given “significant leeway.” Id. 
Appellant’s due process argument should also be rejected because Appellant has failed to 
demonstrate any prejudice from the ALJs’ rulings. The Court has recently reiterated that a “party 
seeking reversal of a Commission order cannot meet its burden of establishing prejudicial error 
without furnishing an argument or citing evidence showing that it has suffered a particularized 
harm.” In re the Application of Ohio Power Co., for Authority to Establish a Std. Service Offer 
Pursuant to R.C. 4928.143, in the Form of an Elec. Security Plan, Slip Opinion No. 2025-Ohio-
3034, at ¶ 16-18, citing In re Complaint of Buckeye Energy Brokers, Inc. v. Palmer Energy Co., 
2014-Ohio-1532, ¶ 23-24. Generalized claims of harm are simply not enough. The Commission 
33 
appropriately found that Appellant demonstrated no prejudice, particularly considering the ample 
latitude afforded during cross-examination. Appellant presented its own witnesses, submitted 
exhibits, filed briefs, and sought rehearing. This Court should uphold the Commission’s decision. 
Appellant also argues that the Commission failed to properly articulate in its Order, as 
required under R.C. 4903.09, the basis for its decision upholding the ALJs’ evidentiary rulings. 
This argument should again be rejected. As discussed previously, R.C. 4903.09 requires that the 
Commission file “findings of fact and written opinions setting forth the reasons prompting the 
decisions arrived at, based upon said findings of fact.” R.C. 4903.09. The Commission fulfilled 
this requirement. The Commission identified the specific rulings at issue, explained why the 
questioning was duplicative or improperly founded, found the ALJs’ parameters reasonable, and 
reaffirmed those conclusions on rehearing. See Order ¶¶ at 28–31, Appellant’s App. at 149-151; 
Entry on Rehearing at ¶ 61, Appellant’s App. at 267. This issue was thoroughly considered at the 
Commission level, satisfying the statutory requirements and allowing meaningful appellate 
review.  
The ALJs acted within their discretion under Adm. Code 4901-1-27(B) to appropriately 
regulate cross-examination. Appellant was afforded extensive opportunity to question witnesses 
and fully participate in this case. The Commission provided detailed reasoning and found no 
prejudice. No due process violation and no violation of R.C. 4903.09 occurred. For these 
reasons, Appellant’s sixth proposition of law should be rejected, and the Commission’s Order 
affirmed. 
CONCLUSION 
The Commission’s Order approving Schedule DCT should be affirmed. The Commission 
confronted a significant and emerging issue in Ohio’s public utility landscape—the rapid 
34 
upcoming influx of data centers with unprecedented load—and addressed it squarely, 
thoroughly, and lawfully. The record amply supports the Commission’s finding that data centers 
are unique and that a distinct tariff, Schedule DCT, is therefore neither unjust nor discriminatory. 
To the contrary, it is essential for protection of all Ohioans and to ensure the integrity of the grid. 
Throughout the proceeding, the Commission acted within its statutory authority, adhered to 
governing administrative law, and exercised its expertise in a reasonable and lawful manner. 
The Commission’s decision should be affirmed.

Q. 
What is the regulatory principle of cost causation? 
10 
A. 
The principle of cost causation is that those who cause a cost to be incurred 
11 
should pay for such costs. The principle does not require perfect apportionment of 
12 
all costs to the cost causer. Practically speaking, perfect cost causation is rarely 
13 
achieved, for several reasons. Among other things, (i) there are often many causes 
14 
for a cost, (ii) precisely identifying who caused a cost may be impossible, a 
15 
judgment call, or administratively infeasible, (iii) it might be impossible or 
16 
administratively burdensome to design rates that perfectly mimic cost causation, 
17 
(iv) cost causation can be assessed in the short run, long run, or something in 
18 
between, with different results, (v) cost causers may cease to be customers before 
19 
they have paid for all costs that they have caused, and (vi) other important 
20 
regulatory principles can conflict with cost causation. Thus, the goal is to assign 
21 
costs to cost causers as best as possible, taking into account these limitations and 
22 
balancing other important regulatory principles.

2 
A. 
It is an important regulatory principle that customers should only pay for utility 
3 
investments that are used and useful in providing utility service. Utilities need to 
4 
“right size” their investments. In other words, ratepayers should not be required to 
5 
pay higher charges if a utility over builds or gold plates its capital investments 
6 
(regardless of whether the utility does so prudently and with good intent). 
7 
 
8 
As I explained above, this proceeding arose because AEP Ohio is facing the 
9 
potential for unprecedented load growth, driven almost entirely by new data 
10 
centers. AEP Ohio knows that it has 5,000 MW of signed data center load, and it 
11 
should be able to reasonably plan for that load growth. But the speculative nature 
12 
of the additional 30,000 MW of potential data center load is a different story. 
13 
Without a data center tariff, it would be challenging, if not impossible, to 
14 
accurately forecast load growth in AEP Ohio’s service territory. In a worst-case 
15 
scenario, AEP Ohio or other transmission owners might pursue transmission 
16 
upgrades designed to accommodate the entire 30,000 MW—even if it is highly 
17 
unlikely that all 30,000 MW of potential data center load materializes—at 
18 
potentially astronomical cost to customers. Transmission could be overbuilt, and 
19 
AEP Ohio ratepayers, including residential consumers, small businesses, and 
20

21 
transmission projects. 
22 
 
23 
39
 
39

1 
improving the accuracy of load forecasts. The terms of the proposed Data Center 
2 
Tariff—higher minimum demand charges, long-term contracts, exit fees, 
3 
minimum load ramps—ensure that only data centers who are truly committed to 
4

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 
1 
 
2 
54. 
Q. 
Can you briefly summarize why the 10/23 Stipulation should be approved 
3 
and the 10/10 Stipulation should be rejected? 
4 
A. 
Yes. As I explained above, Staff has been guided in this case by a desire to 
5 
improve reliability, certainty, and accountability, all for the benefit of ratepayers, 
6 
in addressing the challenges of new data center load. As is often the case in 
7 
Commission proceedings, competing interests must be balanced. The 10/23 
8 
Stipulation is a reasonable compromise that balances the interests of data centers 
9 
and non-data-center customers. Data centers want to locate in Ohio on terms and 
10 
conditions that are fair and reasonable to them. Non-data-center customers want 
11 
protection and assurances that their bills will not become unaffordable because 
12 
they are paying for new infrastructure that is built to accommodate data center 
13 
projects. 
14 
 
15 
The 10/23 Stipulation provides a substantially better balance of interests than the 
16 
10/10 Stipulation: 
17 
• 
By proposing a tariff that applies only to data centers, the 10/23 
18 
Stipulation seeks a data-center-specific solution to an issue being driven 
19 
by data centers. The 10/10 Stipulation, in contrast, casts a wider net, 
20 
subjecting Ohio manufacturers and other non-data-center industrial 
21 
customers to a new tariff and its new obligations. 
22 
59
 
59 
• 
The 10/23 Stipulation includes long-term contracts, coupled with a three-
1 
year exit fee. The 10/10 Stipulation, in contrast, includes long-term 
2 
contracts with either a one-year exit fee or no exit fee at all. 
3 
• 
The 10/23 Stipulation increases minimum demand charges for data center 
4 
customers, with the largest data centers paying a minimum demand 
5 
charge of 85%. To borrow a phrase from AEP Ohio, this gives data 
6 
centers appropriate “skin in the game.” In contrast, many large data 
7 
centers under the 10/10 Stipulation could pay a minimum demand charge 
8 
of just 75%, which shifts too much risk to non-data-center customers. 
9 
• 
Under the 10/10 Stipulation, every data center under 50 MW would be 
10 
exempt from the new tariff. This shifts too much risk to non-data-center 
11 
ratepayers and creates substantial uncertainty in load forecasting. The 
12 
10/23 Stipulation’s 25 MW threshold is more reasonable. 
13 
• 
The 10/10 Stipulation has a massive loophole allowing data centers to 
14 
participate in AEP Ohio’s transmission pilots, which could allow data 
15 
centers to avoid the minimum demand charges that are essential to protect 
16 
other ratepayers. 
17 
• 
The higher load ramp minimums under the 10/23 Stipulation (50%, 65%, 
18 
80%, 90%) compared to the 10/10 Stipulation (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%) 
19 
more reasonably assign costs to the cost causers: data centers. 
20 
 
21 
When viewed as a package, the 10/10 Stipulation is skewed toward the interests 
22 
of data centers. It allows them to avoid financial responsibility for the costs that 
23 
60
 
60 
they cause and shifts far too much risk to non-data-center customers. The 10/23 
1 
Stipulation, in contrast, tends to the needs of both data centers and non-data-
2 
center customers alike. It should be approved without modification because it 
3 
passes the Commission’s three-part test and is superior to the 10/10 Stipulation.
