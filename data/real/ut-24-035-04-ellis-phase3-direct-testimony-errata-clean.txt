56   Q.   WHY IS WILDFIRE LIABILITY SELF-INSURANCE AN IMPORTANT

57       TOPIC?

58   A.    Across the Western US, the risk of catastrophic wildfires is increasing. At

59          times, utilities have been found liable for damages and injuries arising

60         from wildfires caused by their equipment or operations. The costs

61         associated with these liabilities can approach levels commensurate with a

62              utility’s enterprise value, sufficient to put them into bankruptcy, which can

63        be costly and disruptive not just for the utility’s shareholders, but its

64         customers, as well.     Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                     Page 4

65        As a matter of prudency in protecting the interests of both sets of

66          stakeholders, Western utilities are increasingly seeking to procure

67         commercial wildfire liability insurance. The costs of that insurance have

68        been rising rapidly over the last several years, driven by a number of

69           factors, partly because utility-caused wildfires have been increasing in

70         frequency and severity, but also because insured catastrophic losses have

71        been increasing in general, resulting in price increases for numerous types

72           of insurance.

73          Self-insurance can provide a cost-effective alternative to commercial

74          insurance. Self-insurance’s lower cost can afford the utility to procure

75          higher level of coverage. Self-insurance has several additional benefits

76         over commercial insurance. The coverage and terms can be tailored for

77          the specific needs of the utility and its customers, investors, and

78          regulators. For example, a prudency review process, not typically found in

79         commercial insurance, can be incorporated.

80        Perhaps the biggest benefit of self-insurance is that it reduces regret. For

81          catastrophic risk coverage, the cost of insurance is, inevitably, based on a

82          highly uncertain, and typically conservative, estimate of the true risk.

83        Commercial insurance is an annual cost that is paid regardless of whether

84        a claim is ever filed. While, if there is a claim, the utility and its customers

85             will be glad the utility purchased insurance, if there are no claims, there

86             will undoubtedly be questions about whether the insurance was

87          worthwhile.      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                     Page 5

 88          Self-insurance can reduce the potential for regret. Self-insurance typically

 89           entails building a reserve, equal to the level of desired coverage, in an

 90          interest-bearing account. While there is some cost entailed in building the

 91          reserve, if it is never used, the funds can be returned to their provider.

 92             Utility wildfire liability costs are on the order of $0.40 per dollar of

 93         coverage.2 If the utility’s actual risk is materially lower, it could be possible

 94           to fund an equivalent insurance reserve fund in under three years. There

 95             is an up-front cost to establish the reserve, but afterward, as long as the

 96          actual risk is lower than what is implied in the cost of commercial

 97         insurance – 40% – self-insurance will be more cost-effective.

 98           In short, given how material wildfire liability insurance costs are to overall

 99           rates, self-insurance is a compelling win-win-win opportunity for the utility,

100              its customers, and state regulators.

168       WILDFIRE LOSS PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTION ANALYSIS

169   Q.   PLEASE DESCRIBE YOUR PROBABILISTIC ANALYSIS OF

170        PACIFICORP’S WILDFIRE LOSS DISTRIBUTION.

171   A.     In order to better understand and investigate some of the questions

172          involved in assessing the potential benefits of self-insurance, I created a

173           probabilistic analytical model, based on public sources, of PacifiCorp’s

174            wildfire liability risk. The purpose of the model is not to put a precise figure

175        on PacifiCorp’s liability. Rather, it is intended to help identify key      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                     Page 9

176          considerations in comparing self- and commercial insurance and in

177         designing a self-insurance program.

178   Q.  HOW DID YOU CONSRUCT THE MODEL?

179   A.    The model was built in an Excel spreadsheet. It starts with a hypothetical,

180            illustrative probabilistic distribution of expected wildfire liability costs in

181        each of PacifiCorp’s six states.

182   Q.   WHAT IS A PROBABILISTIC DISTRIBUTION?

183   A.   A probabilistic distribution expresses the likelihood, in percentage terms,

184           of a loss of a given size in a given year. For the type of illustrative

185          catastrophic risk modeling entailed in my analysis, a commonly used

186           distribution is the generalized Pareto distribution (GPD).3 It is simple to

187         implement, with only a handful of required parameters. The required input

188         parameters were estimated from publicly available sources on past utility

189            wildfire losses and general understanding of catastrophic risk’s “thick

190            tailed” nature.

191   Q.   WHAT DO YOU MEAN BY “THICK-TAILED”?

192   A.    Catastrophic risk is characterized by infrequent but extreme events that

193          drive up the overall expected (or average) loss. The GPD has a parameter

194            for the degree of this “thickness,” which makes it useful in assessing

195          catastrophic risk like wildfire liability.

196

197   Q.  HOW DID YOU IMPLEMENT THE GPD?

198   A.    Separate distributions were developed for each state then aggregated into

199        a PacifiCorp composite. This composite was then used in a Monte Carlo

200          simulation of a hypothetical self-insurance program: funding terms (e.g.,

201         annual budget), losses, and the resulting fund inflows, outflows, and

202         balances.

203   Q.   WHAT IS A MONTE CARLO SIMULATION?

204   A.    Monte Carlo simulation is an analytical technique that can be used to

205          investigate how various factors can affect a self-insurance reserve fund’s

206            ability to pay claims. Monte Carlo simulation is used to understand the

207         impact of risk and uncertainty in a process that cannot easily be predicted

208        due to the interactions of random variables. It entails repeatedly assigning

209        random sets of values to the key uncertain variables under consideration

210           to generate multiple sets of results and then aggregating those results to

211          obtain a probability distribution for the resulting outcomes.4

212   Q.   WHAT DOES YOUR MODEL ALLOW YOU TO DO?

213   A.    Analyzing the probabilistic wildfire liability distribution in a Monte Carlo

214          simulation can provide insights to help inform the PSC and PacifiCorp on

215          several critical design considerations for self-insurance program,

216           including:

226   Q.   WHAT ARE YOUR KEY FINDINGS ABOUT RISK

227       CHARACTERIZATION?

228   A.    The key finding is that, even though the risk of a major wildfire loss

229         exceeding any reasonable proposed coverage limit in an individual year is

230          low, the probability of exceeding the coverage limit at least once in a multi-

231         year period (e.g., 10-15 years) is quite high.

232   Q.   THAT’S A LITTLE COUNTERINTUITIVE. PLEASE EXPLAIN FURTHER.

233   A.     Wildfire is characterized by infrequent but very large losses. Risk is often

234         expressed in terms of average time between events – once-per-x-years –

235            or, equivalently, with 1/x% probability or 1 at the – 1/x% percentile. For

236         example, a loss expected to occur once every 20 years occurs with a 5%

237           probability or at the 95th percentile.

238        Even though the probability of a loss in a single year is low, the risk that a

239           low-probability, large-loss event can occur at least once over a multi-year

240          period, which is the risk against which a self-insurance fund is intended to      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 12

241          insure, can be significantly higher, depending on the length of the forecast

242          horizon.

243          Building on the previous example, the probability that a once-in-20-years

244         event will occur at least once over a 2-year period can be expressed as

245       100% minus the probability of no events occurring over two years, or

246       100% – (100 – 5%) x (100 – 5%) = 9.75%. Over a 5-year period, the risk is

247       100% – (100 – 5%)5 = 22.6%; that is, even for a risk as low as 5%, the

248           probability that it will occur at least once over a five-year period is 23%,

249           rising to 40%, 54%, and 64% over 10, 15, and 20 years, respectively.

250   Q.   WHAT ARE THE IMPLICATIONS FOR SELF-INSURANCE?

251   A.    What this means for self-insurance is that, if, for example, PacifiCorp

252        wanted to insure for a once-in-20-years loss, and that 5% loss probability

253         corresponded to $1 billion, over a 15-year forecast horizon, the probability

254           of a loss exceeding the fund would be 54%. Given a greater than even

255        chance of exhausting the fund, the PSC and PacifiCorp will want to

256          proactively plan for such an eventuality.

257   Q.   WHAT ARE YOUR KEY FINDINGS ABOUT COST ALLOCATION

258      AMONG STATES?

259   A.   Due to their differing physical and legal environments, the six PacifiCorp

260           states’ relative contributions to the aggregate risk profile differ significantly

261         from typical cost allocation methods. Commonly used cost allocation      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 13

262        methods tend to scale with physical plant, costs, or revenue.5 Although the

263      GPD model is illustrative, it demonstrates that just a few key factors that

264         are not captured in traditional cost allocation methodologies result in

265           relative risk contributions dramatically different than what would occur

266         under traditional methods – with differences varying by as much as a

267           factor of 3.5x.

268                It would be more fair to customers to allocate the cost of self-insurance –

269         both initial fund contributions and replenishment after a loss – based on

270            risk instead of the typical utility cost allocation methodologies. After a loss,

271                  it might be thought that the state that incurred the loss should bear sole

272           responsibility for replenishing the fund. But this both runs counter to the

273           risk-pooling objective of insurance and could be simply unaffordable for

274          the customers of the state in question.

275         Risk-based allocation would help address potential moral hazard

276         concerns. Moral hazard can arise if a utility in one state, recognizing that it

277             is paying into the insurance fund less than its expected losses,

278          underinvests in wildfire mitigation in the knowledge that utilities in other

279          states will bear a disproportionate share of a potential loss.

280   Q.   YOUR WHITEPAPER DISCUSSES THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN

281      COVERAGE LIMIT AND EXPECTED COVERAGE. PLEASE EXPLAIN.





                     5 See, e.g., https://libertyutilities.com/uploads/CAM%20January%201%202017.pdf.      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 14

282   A.    Coverage limit is the more familiar. It’s the maximum amount the insurer

283             will pay out, typically per event or per year.

284         Expected coverage is a probabilistic concept. When insurance is bought

285        and sold, neither party expects the actual coverage to equal the coverage

286             limit every year. If that were so, the insurer would need to charge a

287        premium equal to at least 100% of the premium just to cover the cost of

288          claims.

289          Rather, both parties expect most years to have few or no claims. As a

290            result, the expected coverage – the amount of claims actually paid – is

291           typically a fraction of the coverage limit.

292   Q.   WHY IS THAT IMPORTANT FOR WILDFIRE LIABILITY INSURANCE?

293   A.    There is an additional consideration with insurance for catastrophic risks

294            like wildfire liability. For these risks, the probability distribution is skewed

295          (not symmetrical), with zero losses below the 50th percentile and

296         extremely large losses at the very highest percentiles (99%+). The dollar

297        amounts of losses increase dramatically as their frequency declines.

298         Consequently, expected losses are small relative to the large losses

299         insurance is intended to cover. For example, in my illustrative model, the

300          99th-percentile loss, $1.66 billion, is 61% larger than the 98th percentile,

301         $1.03 billion.

302   Q.   WHAT ARE THE IMPLICATIONS OF THIS SKEWNESS FOR WILDFIRE

303        LIABILITY INSURANCE.      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 15

304   A.    The loss probability’s skewness has two important implications. The first is

305           that insurance, whether commercial or self-, will go unused most of the

306          time. When there is a loss, though, it will likely exceed the coverage limit.

307        As a result, the expected loss covered by insurance will vary with the

308         coverage limit. Second, for self-insurance to fulfill its intended purpose of

309          insuring against rare but extreme losses, the coverage limit will need to be

310           significantly greater than the mean (or expected) loss.

311          This phenomenon has two opposing effects. As just explained, as the fund

312           target increases, a greater share of expected losses is covered; insurance

313             is doing more of its job. On the flip side, as the target increases, the

314         expected drawdown, as a share of the fund target, declines. The fund is

315          increasingly idle with respect to its intended purpose of covering wildfire

316          losses (although it would earn investment income). At the same time,

317         though, because the expected drawdown is low, the expected effective

318          cost of insurance, as a percentage of coverage (the average rate on line,

319          or ROL), declines.

320   Q.   ASIDE FROM POTENTIAL COST SAVINGS, ARE THERE ANY

321       IMPORTANT DIFFERENCES BETWEEN SELF- AND COMMERCIAL

322       INSURANCE?

323   A.    Perhaps the most important difference between self- and commercial

324         insurance is the availability of funds for coverage. When a commercial

325         insurance policy is bought, the insurer is expected, and is required by      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 16

326           regulation, to have the funds available to pay claims up to the coverage

327         amount.

328         With self-insurance, there are circumstances under which the funds

329          available may fall short of the intended coverage limit. This can happen if

330        a cap is imposed on annual payments to the fund that is lower than the

331         coverage target – both in the first few years of the fund, when the

332         cumulative sum of annual payments is less than the target, and later, if

333          there are losses that exceed the annual customer payment cap plus

334         investment gains. The probability that the reserve fund will fall short of an

335          insured loss – i.e., below the target coverage limit – depends on a number

336           of factors beyond the distribution of losses, including:

337             •  Fund coverage target

338             •  Annual payment cap relative to fund target (i.e., the replenishment

339                  rate)

340             •  Fund balance investment returns

341             •  Increase (or decrease) in expected losses over time, due to cost

342                 escalation, utility risk-reduction efforts, changes in law, etc.

343   Q.  HOW BIG ARE THESE POTENTIAL COVERAGE SHORTFALLS?

344   A.    The Monte Carlo simulation was used to examine a reserve fund’s ability

345           to cover insured losses at different annual payment caps. Intuitively, if

346          there’s a commitment to fully replenish the fund after every loss, then

347       100% of insured claims (i.e., claims less than the target in a given year)

348         would be covered by the fund. As the annual payment limit is reduced –      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 17

349            to, say, annual payments capped at 1/3, ¼, or 1/5 of the target (equivalent

350           to filling the initial fund over 3 to 5 years) – the probability of such

351           shortfalls increases, and the expected insured losses that are covered by

352          the fund declines.

353         For example, in my simulation, with 2-year funding, the expected coverage

354            in the first 2 years is about 8% less than the coverage target. With 5-year

355          funding, the expected shortfall is 21% reduction. These are expected

356            shortfalls, based on a probabilistic assessment. It is far more likely than

357          not that there will be no shortfall with the first five years.

358        The erosion in expected coverage with slower funding rates will differ

359          during the fund’s initial build-up phase – the number of years it would take

360           to fill the fund at the annual payment cap, assuming no losses – and

361          afterward.

362   Q.   WHAT ARE THE IMPLICATIONS OF THIS EXPECTED COVERAGE

363       EROSION WHEN CONSIDERING SELF-INSURANCE?

364   A.    The erosion in coverage arising from a cap on annual payments below the

365           target means that a pre-funded self-insurance reserve is not a complete

366           substitute for commercial insurance. Commercial insurance pays up to the

367         coverage limit every year, even if there are losses in successive years.6

368           Full replenishment would replicate commercial insurance. But full

369         replenishment is essentially committing to pay losses, up to the cap, as

370         they are incurred. From the perspective of customer payments into the

371          fund, full replenishment is equivalent to having no insurance (“paying as

372        you go”), but with the additional burden of paying, up-front, for the initial

374   Q.   PLEASE EXPLAIN HOW THE COVERAGE LIMIT AND CUSTOMER

375      PAYMENT CAP AFFECT THE ALLOCATION OF WILDFIRE LIABILITY

376        RISK.

377   A.    Self-insurance does not change the underlying risk exposure. It only

378        changes the allocation of that risk among various parties, as well as the

380            profits and the time-value-of-money for shifting the incidence of that risk

382         annual losses can far exceed any feasible reserve, and expected losses

383         are $170 million/year, equivalent to approximately 3% of PacifiCorp’s total

384         revenue, whether they are commercially insured, self-insured, or not

385          insured at all7.

386        To self-insure to these levels of expected losses implies that PacifiCorp’s

387         customers would be expected to pay at least $170 million, on average,

388         every year. Conversely, capping annual payments at a level below this

389        amount necessarily implies that PacifiCorp customers are not expected to

392                It may be the case that, for policy reasons, Utah and other Western states

403   Q.   WHAT IF WE WANTED TO MAKE THE FUND SELF-SUSTAINING?

404      HOW MUCH WOULD CUSTOMERS NEED TO CONTRIBUTE TO MAKE

405      SURE THE FUND’S INVESTMENT RETURNS COVERED EXPECTED

407   A.   A self-financing fund, large enough to generate investment income

408           sufficient to cover expected insured losses, could, in principle, more

409          closely replicate commercial insurance. To be self-financing, the fund’s

410         annual investment proceeds would need to cover expected annual losses,

411          plus any expected growth in those losses over time. There is a

412         mathematical formula to calculate the size of the fund required, assuming      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 20

413          the investment return and growth rates are constant, and that losses grow

414           at the same rate every year:

415        Fund size =annual insured losses / (investment return rate – loss growth

416            rate). To illustrate, this formula can be applied to a hypothetical proposed

417         fund target of $1 billion with expected insured losses of $100 million.

418         Expected investment returns for property and casualty insurers are on the

419          order of 4.0%.8 For the loss growth rate, given the increasing frequency

420        and severity of wildfires and the shifting legal landscape, it is reasonable

421           to assume growth faster than overall inflation, say 1% more than assumed

422            inflation of 2.5%.The fund size would therefore be $100 million / (4.0% –

423         3.5%) = $20 billion.

424       A fund this size is larger than PacifiCorp’s equity capitalization so is likely

425          unaffordable.9 Also, there is no guarantee the fund will perform as

426         expected: actual losses might exceed the average (as is expected, with

427       some probability, in any probabilistic forecast) or investment returns might

428        be lower.

429       On the plus side, a $20-billion reserve fund would be able to cover over a

430          very high percentage of all expected losses, not just those up to the $1-

431            billion coverage limit.

439       ALTERNATIVE APPROACH TO SELF-INSURANCE

440   Q.   WHAT ARE YOUR KEY CONCERNS ABOUT A SELF-INSURANCE

441       RESERVE FUND?

442   A.    The goal of self-insurance is to replicate commercial insurance as closely

443        as possible: exchanging a fixed annual payment for coverage of

444          infrequent, unpredictable large losses. A self-insurance reserve fund isn’t

445        an ideal substitute, though, because it can be exhausted when there are

446          sequential large losses over two or more years, materially eroding its

447           effective coverage, particularly in the initial years as the reserve is being

448            built up.

449           Ideally, the potential early-year shortfall could be bridged by continuing to

450         purchase commercial insurance for the gap between the reserve fund

451         balance and coverage target. Commercial insurance costs on the order of

452         $0.40 per dollar of coverage.10 Assuming a $1-billion coverage target with

                          California Edison to Recover Specific Insurance Costs Recorded in Its Wildfire      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 22

453        an annual customer payment cap of $250 million, the cost of commercial

454          insurance, $0.40 per dollar of coverage x $1 billion = $400 million, would

455        exceed the $250-million dollar customer payment cap, leaving nothing to

456          contribute to the reserve fund.

457       An additional shortcoming of a self-insurance reserve fund is that,

458        because of the skewness in the distribution of losses, expected loss

459         coverage at typical coverage levels is a relatively small fraction of the

460          fund’s total size, so the fund mostly sits idle.

465          revolving credit facility (RCF) that can be drawn upon to cover insurance

466          claims as they are incurred, repaid over time, and redrawn as needed.11

467        To reduce the financing cost of the RCF and remove the liability from the

468              utilities’ balance sheet, the RCF could be backed by a dedicated customer

469          surcharge, similar to how a reserve fund would be held outside the

470             utility.12 Once drawn, the RCF could be repaid through a securitized bond,

499   A.    The cost of a revolving credit facility has two components: the interest rate

500        on the outstanding balance (whether in the RCF or in a separate

501           securitization), tied to the credit quality of the securitized customer

502        payment stream; and a lower reservation or commitment fee on the

503        undrawn balance. Utility securitizations typically have very high credit

504           ratings, so the interest rate and reservation fee are assumed to be

505           relatively low: 5.1% and 0.2%, respectively, with the former reflecting

506        Secured Overnight Financing Rate (4.8% as of October 202414) plus a

507        0.3% margin.

545   Q.   WHAT IS THE MOST IMPORTANT ADVANTAGE OF AN RCF?

546   A.   A reserve fund requires relatively high customer payments in the first few

547          years, in order to build up the fund so it is available in the event of a major

548           loss. In contrast, with an RCF, customers pay for losses after the fact, so

549          the payments can be spread out over a longer period of time. An RCF

550          therefore allows for a higher coverage target given a maximum annual

551        payment cap. As explained above, the higher the target, the higher the

552         share of total losses that can be insured.      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 27

553                If we assume PacifiCorp has an annual budget for insurance, a higher

554           target can be maintained by setting a longer replenishment period. A

555          longer replenishment period reduces the RCF’s ability to cover multiple

556          sequential losses, but the Monte Carlo analysis reveals that this effect is

557         outweighed by the higher coverage limit.

558        The Monte Carlo analysis was used to determine the expected coverage

559        and customer cost at different insurance targets, given a maximum annual

560        payment constraint.18 From this analysis, several observations emerge.

561        The first is that longer replenishment periods (i.e., RCF repayment

562         schedules) allow a higher target. In practice, the replenishment period will

563            likely be dictated by the financial institution(s) providing the facility or the

564          tenor of the securitization bond. The key implication is that the amount of

565         coverage can vary, even subject to an annual payment cap. As discussed

566         above, the higher the coverage target, the greater the share of total losses

567           that can be self-insured.

569          insured losses that are not covered increases as well. An RCF is not a

570         complete substitute for commercial insurance. Whenever there is a claim

571          paid by the RCF that exceeds the annual payment cap, the RCF will not

572        be able to cover a future claim that exceeds the undrawn balance on the

573       RCF until the RCF is fully repaid.

584       KEY CONSIDERATIONS, NEXT STEPS, AND CONCLUSION

585   Q.   WHAT SHOULD THE PSC AND PACIFICORP DO NEXT IN THEIR

586       CONSIDERATION OF SELF-INSURANCE?

587   A.     Wildfire liability risk for regulated utilities has some unique characteristics

588           that both raise concerns about insuring this risk and create opportunities

589           to do so more cost-effectively than in other industries. Unlike other types

590           of catastrophic risk, like earthquakes or storms, utilities may sometimes be

591          the cause of wildfires, which amplifies their potential financial exposure, in

592          the form of liability for third-party damages and associated penalties. The

593           potential for moral hazard, always present in risk-pooling, is therefore

594          correspondingly greater.

595          At the same time, utilities’ unique ability to pass through legitimate wildfire

596              liability costs after the fact means they need not pay in advance for risks      Phase III OCS-6D Ellis           24-035-04                    Page 29

597           that may never materialize to effectively insure against them. They can

598          instead choose to finance only their actual wildfire liability costs as they

599         are incurred. This unusual financial flexibility creates opportunities for

600             utilities to pool and insure risk at much lower cost.

601   Q.   WHAT SHOULD THE PSC AND PACIFICORP DO NEXT IN THEIR

602       CONSIDERATION OF SELF-INSURANCE?

603   A.    Self-insurance is a promising vehicle to manage PacifiCorp’s wildfire

604              liability risk in a manner the protects the interests of both customers and

605          shareholders. The PSC and PacifiCorp will want to research and address

606        a variety of critical issues in assessing and designing a wildfire liability

607          self-insurance program. Fortunately, most of the qualitative issues have

608        been previously examined elsewhere – for utilities specifically or within the

609         insurance industry more generally – from risk-pooling and moral hazard to

610        managing an insurance captive to utility and regulatory organizational

611        demands. In contrast, the quantitative and financial considerations will be

612         unique to PacifiCorp’s risk profile and financial position, as well as the

613        PSC’s and other state regulators’ view on how, and how much, wildfire

614              liability risk should be allocated to customers.
